Two halves

Said once, plainly, right after the cold open and before the problem lands. There are two halves to all of this: cohering our thinking, and coordinating our action. This whole talk is the first half. The second, how work gets organised and tracked, is a talk of its own. Today is about the thinking.

The closing line makes that dependency explicit: the tree of action would build on the tree of change, not stand apart from it.

Ancestors of the Ideas

Eliyahu Goldratt’s Theory of Constraints diagnoses an organisation with literal cause-and-effect trees. Barbara Minto’s Pyramid Principle structures an argument the same way — one governing thought, branching into what supports it. Dettmer’s Logical Thinking Process, thirty years later, develops and summarises Goldratt’s own trees. Nothing here depends on knowing any of it.

Always in this order

  1. 1 Where does it fit?
  2. 2 Gap or disagreement?
  3. 3 So what, and who owns it?

Question one is the easy part, and it is the only part a machine does well. Two and three are the group’s work.

The three questions

This slide stays up while you walk one or two contributions through. Each number lights as you reach it.

1 — where does it fit? The tree proposes a location. Easy part.
2 — gap or disagreement? Is it under a condition nobody is holding, or in tension with a link? Now it is doing work on the group’s reasoning.
3 — so what? If the room broadly agrees: what is the next action, and who might own it?

If the placement is weak, say so, place it by hand, and move on. A wrong first guess that a person corrects in five seconds is still the system working.
