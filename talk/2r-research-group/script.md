# Script

Second-Renaissance-first. The room's problem, then trees, then the onramp, then they try it, then the ask. ~60–75 min with the live block; ~35 min without.

Two layers below: a **skeleton** (what each beat has to land) and then the **full script** (what to actually say). The full script is prose where the wording matters and bullets where it's staging or where the room's input fills the gap.

---

## Skeleton

**0 · Cold open — the question and where it went (3 min)**
- A few months ago, in a sense-making session, someone asked: what is the goal of Second Renaissance, and how would we know if we were moving toward it?
- Good question. Genuinely good hour of talk. Then — nothing downstream. Where did it go?
- That question, and its disappearance, is what this talk is about. It's also literally where this work started.

**1 · What 2R needs, and why it doesn't accumulate (12 min)**
- 2R: a movement for civilizational renewal, largely volunteer, with a real and deliberately-still-evolving thesis. Getting better as more people work on it is the design, not a bug.
- It needs two things. One: **cohering our thinking** — better hypotheses about how renewal happens, sharper arguments for why, the detail researched, the theory of change improved. Two: **coordinating our action** — where do you plug in, how do initiatives move together. Today is about the first. The second is a follow-up talk; I'll name it again later and leave it there.
- The problem with the first: our thinking doesn't accumulate. Threads, calls, docs. New people arrive and re-cover old ground. The research circle reaches the same conclusion twice in three years and both times it evaporates. The same conversation, again.
- Two failure patterns: reasoning doesn't accumulate; and willingness has nowhere to land — people want to help and there's no obvious place to put the help.
- This is not a shortage of intelligence, goodwill, or ideas. It's a shortage of somewhere to put them.
- Why the obvious fix — write it up — doesn't work. Six or seven white papers, ~200k words. A claim in the middle of a paragraph has no address. You can't point at it, so you can't object to it precisely, so you can't suggest a small change to it, so nothing accumulates against it.

**2 · The idea: trees, and the conflict under them (12 min)**
- What if the shared thing weren't prose but structure: a top claim broken into sub-claims, all the way down. Every claim has an address. The links are claims too — *this one is necessary for that one*. The reasoning is exposed, not just the conclusions.
- Show the real 2R tree of change: the goal, and the eight things that all have to be true — shared diagnosis, compelling vision, embodied practice, pockets, sangha, movement coherence, institutions, wise transition.
- It's two things at once: a thesis about what has to hold, and an object we can improve. Whole and part stay connected — every leaf traces back to the goal.
- One sentence on lineage: this shape is 40 years old, came out of operations research, is called the Logical Thinking Process; a companion talk does it properly; nothing here needs it.
- The conflict. People want conversation to stay natural — spontaneous, exploratory, relational. Nobody wants to write in formal logic. Accumulation wants structure. They look opposed.
- The false assumption holding them opposed: *that the structure has to be added by the contributor, at the moment of contributing.* That's the only thing making this a dilemma. And it isn't true.

**3 · The move: contribute normally, structure revealed underneath (6 min)**
- Let the conversation be a conversation. Nothing interrupts it.
- Afterwards a machine reads the thread against the tree and proposes addresses: this supports that condition, this challenges that link, this is a claim the tree can't place yet.
- A rotating handful of people decide what actually merges — because one person quietly deciding what the map says is how you get a map nobody trusts.
- Cost of contributing falls back to one sentence, said where you were already saying it. The filing is someone's job; it isn't the contributor's.
- AI is what makes this newly possible: it builds the trees fast from material that already exists, and it connects live discourse to the tree with almost no labour. Two years ago this talk would have ended at "nice idea, too much hand-work."

**4 · Try it (18 min, live)**
- Everyone: ~1,000 characters. Something important 2R may be missing, underestimating, or getting wrong — or could act on. Your words.
- Take one or two live. Three questions in order:
  1. Where does this fit? (placement — the easy, least interesting part)
  2. Does it reveal a gap or a disagreement? (now it's working on the group's reasoning)
  3. So what? (if 2R is underestimating this — what might we actually do?)
- The outcome travels backward: contribution → shared reasoning → bottleneck/opportunity → proposal → action → evidence → better shared reasoning. Not "thanks for your feedback."

**5 · Close (6 min)**
- What you just did is a small instance of a whole method: current reality, goal, the conflict, a future reality, the steps between. Five shapes. Name them, don't teach them.
- Back to the opening question. A shared, living theory of change is what lets you even ask "are we cohering? are we moving?" — carefully, because some of us are wary of that question, and that wariness is worth a conversation of its own.
- The ask: today, contribute something. After today, follow what happens to it. If there's an action you want to own, take it. Help us test whether this makes 2R better at thinking — and later, at doing.
- What's being tested isn't whether the software is clever. It's whether today's contributions improve 2R's reasoning and cause useful things to happen.
- Honest status: the trees are provisional and wrong in places, the reader is a prototype, the merge rules are three sentences and untested. They're public anyway. Find the claim you disagree with, or the one nobody is standing under.

## Full script

### 0 · Cold open

A few months ago, in one of the sense-making sessions, someone asked a simple question. *What is the goal of the Second Renaissance — and how would we know if we were actually moving toward it?*

It was a good question. It got a good hour. People said thoughtful things, some of which I still think about.

And then — as far as I can tell — nothing. No note that changed what we do. No thread that picked it up. If you went looking for the answer that hour produced, there's nowhere to go and look.

I want to talk about that: the question, and the fact that it evaporated. Partly because it's a pattern most of us would recognise. And partly because this — trying to work out what happened to that conversation — is literally where the project I'm going to show you started.

### 1 · What 2R needs, and why it doesn't accumulate

Quick ground, then I'll move fast because you know this better than I do.

The Second Renaissance is a movement for civilizational renewal. It's largely volunteer. It has a real vision and a real theory of change — and both are still evolving, on purpose. The whole premise is that this gets better, sharper, more defensible as more people work on it.

So the movement needs two things that are easy to say and hard to do.

The first is **cohering our thinking**. Better hypotheses about how a renewal could actually happen. Sharper arguments for why it's needed, the kind you could hand to someone who doesn't already agree. The details researched. The theory of change genuinely improved over time, not just restated.

The second is **coordinating our action**. If I want to help, where do I plug in? How do the different initiatives actually move together rather than in parallel? How would we know we're getting somewhere?

Today is about the first one. The second — call it the tree of action — is a whole talk of its own, and I'm going to name it again at the end and then deliberately leave it alone.

Here's the problem with the first one. Our thinking doesn't accumulate.

It's not that people aren't thinking. There's a forum. There are calls. There are documents. But new people show up and re-cover ground that was covered a year ago. A research circle I know of has reached the same conclusion twice in three years — the room agreed both times — and both times, two weeks later, the agreement had nobody's name on it and nothing had changed. Then months later it came round again from the start, because the conditions that produced it were exactly where they'd been.

Anyone who's been around this more than a year has had that conversation three times.

There are two shapes to this. One: **reasoning doesn't accumulate** — an insight leads to more conversation, the context gets lost, and you're back to the same conversation again. Two: **willingness has nowhere to land** — someone says "I want to help," there's no obvious place to put the help, and the willingness just dissipates. Willingness isn't our scarce resource. Somewhere for it to land is.

I want to be careful here. None of this is a shortage of intelligence. Or goodwill. Or ideas. We are not short of ideas.

It's a shortage of somewhere to put them.

And the obvious fix doesn't work. "Write it up properly" gives you six or seven white papers and something like two hundred thousand words. Now: a claim buried in the middle of a paragraph on page forty has no address. You can't point at it. If you can't point at it, you can't disagree with it precisely — and a vague objection to a long document is one nobody can act on, so in the end nobody makes it. You can't suggest a small change to it. Nothing builds against it. The document is a wall, not a scaffold.

### 2 · The idea: trees, and the conflict under them

So here's the move. What if the thing we hold in common isn't prose — it's structure.

You take the top-level claim. You break it into the sub-claims it depends on. You break those down again, as far as is useful. Now every claim has an address — you can point at exactly this one. And the links between claims are themselves claims: *this one is necessary for that one*. So what's on the page isn't just the conclusions, it's the reasoning.

Let me show you a real one. This is the Second Renaissance tree of change — not something I invented for the talk, it's built from the actual corpus.

[SHOW: goal tree. Goal at the top: *co-initiate a Second Renaissance — a conscious transformation of foundational views, values, practices and institutions toward a wiser, weller, regenerative civilization.* Then the eight.]

Eight things all have to be true at once for this to be more than an idea. People have to understand the crises as one crisis — a shared diagnosis. There has to be a compelling picture of the next paradigm. It has to be embodied in actual practice, not just described. There have to be pockets where a new culture is genuinely livable. Those pockets need a wider community around them. That community has to cohere into a movement that can transmit itself. The movement has to reshape institutions. And all of it has to navigate collapse and renewal wisely rather than naively.

Two things about this tree at once. It's a **thesis** — a claim that these eight, in this relationship, are what it takes. And it's an **object we can work on** — every one of those is arguable, and the arguing is the point. Whole and part stay connected: every leaf, however far down, traces back to the goal at the top.

One sentence on where this comes from, because it matters and then it doesn't. The method behind this shape is about forty years old. It came out of operations research — factories, throughput, bottlenecks — and it's called the Logical Thinking Process. There's a companion talk that does it justice. Nothing I'm saying today depends on you knowing any of that, and if the factory smell puts you off, ignore it.

Now — the reason we don't already do this.

There's a real conflict here, and every community that tries to give itself structure runs into it. On one side: people want the conversation to stay natural. Spontaneous, exploratory, relational. Nobody joined a movement for civilizational renewal in order to write in formal logic, and people are rightly suspicious of anything that interrupts a good exchange in order to file it. On the other side: accumulation needs structure. If everything stays purely conversational, the important ideas dissolve into chronology and we're back where we started.

Those two look opposed. Spontaneity versus structure. Pick one.

But there's exactly one assumption holding them in opposition, and once you see it you can't unsee it. The assumption is: **the structure has to be added by the contributor, at the moment of contributing.** That's the only thing that makes this a dilemma.

And it's not true.

### 3 · The move: contribute normally, structure revealed underneath

So don't add the structure at the moment of contributing.

Let the conversation be a conversation. Nothing interrupts it. You post your paragraph on the forum where you were going to post it anyway.

Afterwards — separately — a machine reads the thread against the tree and proposes addresses. This sentence supports that condition. This one challenges that link. This one is a claim the tree currently has nowhere to put — which is itself useful information.

Then a rotating handful of people decide what actually merges into the tree. Not one person, and not the machine on its own — because one party quietly deciding what the map says is exactly how you end up with a map nobody trusts.

What's happened to the cost of contributing? It's fallen back to one sentence, said where you were already saying it. The filing still gets done — it's just somebody's job now, and it isn't the contributor's.

And this is the part that's genuinely new. The reason this is a talk in 2026 and not 2016: AI. It can build a first draft of these trees quickly from material that already exists — the white papers, the forum, the transcripts. And it can connect live discourse to the tree with almost no hand-labour. Take AI out of this picture and the honest ending is "lovely idea, far too much manual work, nobody will keep it up." Put it back in and the arithmetic changes.

### 4 · Try it

I'd rather show you than keep asserting it. So let's actually do it.

[INTERACTIVE — setup]
- Everyone takes a few minutes and writes about a thousand characters. The prompt: *something important the Second Renaissance may be missing, underestimating, or getting wrong — or something we could act on.* Your words, exactly as you'd say them. No need to make it tidy.
- [Collect. Pick one or two — ideally one "gap" and one "disagreement".]

[INTERACTIVE — walk one or two through, in this order]

1. **Where does this fit?** The system places it against the tree. This is the easy part, and honestly the least interesting — it's semantic matching. Fine. Keep going.
2. **Does it reveal a gap or a disagreement?** This is where it starts doing work. Is this contribution pointing at a condition nobody's currently holding? Or is it in tension with a link we've got drawn? Either way it's now acting on the group's reasoning, not just sitting next to it.
3. **So what?** If the room is right that 2R is underestimating — say — intergenerational transmission, then what do we actually do? What's the next action, and who might own it?

[Then show the shape of the outcome:]

Notice the direction this travels. It starts as a contribution in a conversation. It becomes a piece of shared reasoning at an address. That surfaces a bottleneck or an opportunity. That turns into a proposal. The proposal turns into an action. The action produces evidence. The evidence improves the shared reasoning.

It does not end with "thanks for your feedback."

### 5 · Close

What you just did is a small instance of a whole method. It has five moves, and I'm going to name them, not teach them. You start from **current reality** — why conversations don't accumulate. You define the **goal** — a group that gets smarter and turns thinking into action. You find the **conflict** — natural conversation versus necessary structure. You describe a **future reality** — contributions becoming cumulative reasoning. And then you work out the **steps** from here to there. Current reality, goal, conflict, future, prerequisites. Five shapes.

And it lets me go back to the question from the start of this talk. *How would we know if we were moving toward what we're trying to do?* A shared, living theory of change is the thing that lets you even ask that — carefully. I know "goal" and "progress" and "are we moving" are loaded words in this room, and that some of you think a renewal isn't the kind of thing you march toward. That's a real disagreement and I'd genuinely rather have it out loud than design around it. But you can't have it at all if the thing you'd be arguing about only exists as prose nobody can point at.

So here's the ask.

Today: contribute something. Actually write the thousand characters.

After today: follow what happens to it. See if it lands anywhere.

If there's an action that comes out of it that you want to own — take it.

And help us test the real question, which is not whether the software is clever. It's whether today's contributions actually improve the movement's reasoning, and actually cause useful things to happen.

Last thing, so I'm not overselling. The trees are provisional and wrong in several places. The thing that reads the threads is a prototype. The rules for what merges are about three sentences long and have never been tested by anyone genuinely annoyed. All of it is public anyway.

Find the claim you disagree with. Or find the one nobody's standing under.

The Second Renaissance has never been short of thinking. It's been short of somewhere to put it.
