# Piece 1 — Beat Sheet

**Answers:** why don't your fixes stick?
**Payload:** a system's output is set by one constraint; optimising every part is not
optimising the whole; local efficiency can actively harm global output.
**Hands off with:** "So — find the constraint. But *how?* That's a thinking problem."

**Title candidates:** "Everybody's Busy. Nothing Moves." · "Where It's Actually Stuck" ·
"The Weakest Link" · "One Thing Is Stuck"

**Source tags:** `[D ch1 pN]` = Dettmer ch1, printed page · `[syn PN]` = existing
synthesis, Part N · `[RP]` = Rufus, 2026-07-30 outflow.

**Interactive beats (3):** `INT-1` the hire · `INT-2` the busy question · `INT-3` the migrating constraint.

## ACT 1 · Constrained toward what? *(short — 3 beats)*

| # | Beat | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Before you can ask what's limiting a system, you have to answer something more basic and more often skipped: **what is it for?** | `[D ch1 p5-7]` `[syn P2]` |
| 1.2 | A system: a collection of interdependent parts that turn inputs into outputs in pursuit of a goal. The last phrase is the one people skip. | `[D ch1 p4]` |
| 1.3 | The clinic's goal is *not* "see patients" — that's activity. It's "people leave healthier." Change the goal and the constraint moves with it. A constraint only exists **relative to a goal.** | `[D ch1 p5]` |
| 1.4 | Ask ten people in one organisation what its goal is and you get ten answers, each defensible. That is not a trivia problem. Until it's settled, every argument about what's broken is really an argument about what "working" would mean. | `[syn P2]` |

## ACT 2 · The chain

| # | Beat | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Picture a chain. Its goal: transmit force. Pull harder and harder. It breaks — where? | `[D ch1 p8]` |
| 2.2 | At its weakest link. Obviously. Stay with the obvious, because it's about to turn subversive: **how many weakest links does a chain have? One.** The strength of the whole chain — dozens of links, tons of steel — is set by that single link. | `[D ch1 p8-9]` `[syn P2]` |
| 2.3 | Now reinforce any *other* link. How much stronger is the chain? Not one bit. You've added weight, cost and effort, and it breaks at exactly the same force, at exactly the same link. **All that improvement, and zero improvement.** | `[D ch1 p9]` |
| 2.4 | This is the whole theory in one sentence: at any moment a system's output is limited by one thing. Everything else is a non-constraint, and improving a non-constraint does nothing — no matter how skilfully it's done. | `[D ch1 p9]` |

> `[FIGURE 2]` **The chain.** Links of visibly varying thickness. Scroll drives a load gauge upward; one link reddens and snaps. Second state: three *other* links thickened, load re-applied, snaps at the same link at the same number. The number is the point — show it twice, unchanged.

## ACT 3 · The busy trap *(the piece's centre of gravity)*

| # | Beat | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | **`INT-2`** — "Every single person in an organisation is working flat out, all day. Is the organisation running at its best?" Two buttons. *Commit before scrolling.* | `[RP]` |
| 3.2 | Almost everyone says yes. Here is Dettmer, flatly: *"If a system is performing as well as it can, not more than one of its component parts will be performing as well as they can. If all parts are performing as well as they can, the system as a whole will not be."* | `[D ch1 p13]` |
| 3.3 | Read it twice. It doesn't say full effort is *unnecessary*. It says full effort everywhere is **positive evidence that the system is not running well.** | — |
| 3.4 | Why: a non-constraint running flat out produces work the constraint cannot absorb. In a factory, that's inventory clogging the floor. In the clinic, it's a waiting room filled faster. On a team, it's a backlog of "finished" work nobody can review. Local efficiency converted into system-wide waste. | `[D ch1 p13]` `[syn P2]` |
| 3.5 | **So a well-run system requires most of its parts to have slack** — to deliberately run below their maximum — so that the one part that limits everything can run flat out. | `[syn P2]` |
| 3.6 | The corollary is brutal. Picture an organisation with a dozen energetic improvement teams, all working hard on things everybody knows need improving. How many are working on the constraint? **At most one.** | `[D ch1 p11]` |

> `[FIGURE 3]` **Twelve teams, one constraint.** Twelve small squares; one lights. The other eleven stay grey. Caption carries the number, not the metaphor.

## ACT 4 · Exploit before you spend

| # | Beat | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Back to the clinic. We know the constraint is the doctor. Intuition now says: hire another doctor. | — |
| 4.2 | Not yet. Watch her day. Roughly two hours of it is spent on things that do not require a medical degree — re-taking histories a nurse already took, chasing notes, printing forms. | after `[D ch1 p14]` exploit |
| 4.3 | **Exploit** means: wring every bit of capacity out of the constraint *as it currently exists*, before spending anything. Move those two hours off her desk and the clinic goes from 20 patients a day to 28. Cost: nothing. | `[D ch1 p14]` |
| 4.4 | **Subordinate** means: everyone else now runs at whatever pace keeps *her* running flat out. Reception deliberately slows. That will feel wrong — it will look like under-performance on every local metric you have. It is the correct decision. | `[D ch1 p14]` |
| 4.5 | Only now, if you still need more, do you **elevate** — spend the money. And when you do, you hire *for the doctor*, not for the loudest queue. | `[D ch1 p15]` |
| 4.6 | Dettmer's warning: organisations that don't think this way "jump straight from Step 1 to Step 4" — from *identify* to *spend*. They incur costs they didn't need to, and skip the free improvement entirely. | `[D ch1 p15]` |
| 4.7 | The five steps, named once, briefly: identify · exploit · subordinate · elevate · **go back — and beware inertia.** | `[D ch1 p14-15]` |

| # | Beat | Source |

| 5.4 | Same shape every time. The question is never *how hard are we pushing?* It's **are we pushing on the thing that's actually stuck?** | `[syn P2]` |

## ACT 6 · It moves

| # | Beat | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Fix the doctor and the clinic doesn't become "fixed." The constraint **moves** — to pharmacy, then to follow-up booking. | `[D ch1 p10]` |
| 6.2 | **`INT-3`** — reader drags the doctor's capacity slider up and watches the red band jump to a different station. The system's output rises, then flattens again somewhere new. | `[RP]` |
| 6.3 | Push far enough and every station outruns the population it serves. Constraint gone? *"Well, not really."* It has moved outside the walls — to the referral hospital, or the catchment itself. Still a constraint. It just now needs a completely different set of skills to attack. | `[D ch1 p10]` |
| 6.4 | Which is why this never finishes. "Yesterday's solution becomes today's historical curiosity." Inertia is the enemy: *we solved that, no need to revisit it* is how a solved problem becomes the next constraint. | `[D ch1 p13]` |

## ACT 7 · The turn — most constraints are invisible

| # | Beat | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | Everything so far has quietly cheated. In the clinic, the constraint was a **person you could point at**, with a number attached. In real organisations it almost never is. | — |
| 7.2 | Most constraints are not machines or people. They're **policies** — a rule, a practice, a habit, a way-we-do-things-here. *Every prescription needs the doctor's countersignature. No appointment over ten minutes. Nothing ships without sign-off. We don't discuss money in this family.* | `[D ch1 p13]` `[syn P2]` |
| 7.3 | Every one was installed for a good reason, usually to solve a problem nobody now remembers. John Gardner: an established organisation is *"a coral reef of procedures that were laid down to achieve some long-forgotten objective."* | `[D ch1 p13]` |
| 7.4 | Two properties that should give you a small chill. **First:** breaking a policy constraint is nearly free — and yields *more* improvement than breaking a physical one. **Second:** they are invisible, because everyone experiences them as normal. | `[D ch1 p13]` |
| 7.5 | A bottleneck machine announces itself with a pile of work in front of it. A bottleneck policy hides inside "that's just how it's done" — defended, sincerely, by good people whose own numbers depend on it. | `[syn P2]` |

## ACT 8 · The handoff

| # | Beat | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 8.1 | So the instruction is simple: find the constraint, and put nearly everything you have into it. | — |
| 8.2 | But if the constraint is usually a policy — invisible, normal-seeming, defended by sincere people, and connected to the symptoms you *can* see by long chains of cause and effect that cross every boundary on the org chart — then **how would you ever find one?** | `[syn P2]` |
| 8.3 | You can't see it. You can't touch it. And your intuition, trained entirely on the visible, keeps pointing at the receptionist. | — |
| 8.4 | Ask five knowledgeable colleagues and you'll get five confident, different answers. On what basis would you decide who's right? Seniority? Confidence? Volume? Most organisations settle it with some blend of authority, anecdote, and whoever tells the most vivid story — which is to say they don't settle it. **They guess.** | `[syn P3]` |
| 8.5 | That is not a management problem. It's a **thinking** problem. And it's the reason a theory about factories ended up producing something much stranger: a set of tools for thinking itself. | `[syn P2]` |
