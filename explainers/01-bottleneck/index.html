A clinic opens at eight. By noon, sixty people are waiting.

Everyone who works there can tell you exactly where the problem is.

They are all pointing at the same desk.

They are all wrong.

Everybody's Busy

Why the thing you can see is almost never the thing that's stuck.

The receptionist at this clinic works through lunch most days. The phone starts before the lights are on. Every complaint letter names her desk. She is, visibly and unarguably, the hardest-working person in the building.

She is also irrelevant to the clinic's output. It treats twenty patients a day, and it has treated twenty patients a day for four years — a number set entirely by something almost nobody in the building is looking at.

This is not a story about a clinic. It is the most reliably counterintuitive idea in management, and once you have seen it you will see it in your work, your team and your week.

Everyone who comes through the clinic passes five stations, in order, and every one has a limit.

Check-in can process sixty people a day.

Triage, forty-five.

The doctor, twenty.

Pharmacy, fifty. Follow-up booking, forty.

Four of the five stations can handle more than twice what the doctor can.

So the clinic treats twenty people a day.

Not eighty-five, which is what its five stations could manage between them. Twenty.
