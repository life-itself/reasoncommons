# Piece 1 — *Everybody's Busy* — shot list

Built in the ProPublica form. Each block is one text unit over one drawing; the drawing
holds the screen while the sentence lands. Target: **median ~16 words per block, ~55
blocks, zero interactions.**

Prose source: `draft.md` (v1, essay form — kept for phrasing, not structure).
Beat logic + source tags: `beats.md`.

`▸` = the drawing on screen for that block.
**§** headings are structural notes, not text on the page.
Word counts in `[ ]`.

## COLD OPEN
*Four sentences, one per screen, over the opening drawing. No headline yet.*

**[10]** A clinic opens at eight. By noon, sixty people are waiting.
▸ Hand-drawn clinic exterior on paper ground. A queue of small figures curls out of the door and down the street. The queue **lengthens as you scroll** — figures drawn in, one by one.

**[11]** Everyone who works there can tell you exactly where the problem is.
▸ Camera pushes inside. Five rooms visible in cutaway — a doll's-house view of the clinic.

**[7]** They are all pointing at the same desk.
▸ Every figure in the building turns; hand-drawn arrows converge on the front desk. The desk is the only thing in colour.

**[4]** **They are all wrong.**
▸ Everything drains to flat grey except the doctor's room, far from the desk, which holds a single small mark of colour.

## NUT GRAPHS
*The only paragraph density in the piece. Three blocks, then never again.*

**[38]** The receptionist at this clinic works through lunch most days. The phone starts before the lights are on. Every complaint letter names her desk. She is, visibly and unarguably, the hardest-working person in the building.

**[41]** She is also irrelevant to the clinic's output. It treats twenty patients a day, and it has treated twenty patients a day for four years — a number set entirely by something almost nobody in the building is looking at.

**[35]** This is not a story about a clinic. It is the most reliably counterintuitive idea in management, and once you have seen it you will see it in your work, your team and your week.

---

## §1 · The five stations

**[16]** Everyone who comes through the clinic passes five stations, in order, and every one has a limit.
▸ The five stations drawn left to right as rough ink boxes: **check-in · triage · doctor · pharmacy · follow-up**. Patients enter as small dots.

**[12]** Check-in can process sixty people a day.
▸ `60` inked above check-in. Dots stream through it easily.

**[6]** Triage, forty-five.
▸ `45` inked above triage.

**[4]** The doctor, twenty.
▸ `20` inked above the doctor. **Dots begin to pool.** The pile in front of the doctor's box grows as you scroll.

**[7]** Pharmacy, fifty. Follow-up booking, forty.
▸ `50` and `40` inked. Those boxes sit half-idle, drawn with visible empty space.

**[13]** Four of the five stations can handle more than twice what the doctor can.
▸ All five capacities visible at once as rough bars. One bar is conspicuously short.

**[9]** So the clinic treats twenty people a day.
▸ A hand-drawn counter at the end of the line rolls up — and **stops dead at 20** while dots keep arriving.

**[14]** Not eighty-five, which is what its five stations could manage between them. Twenty.
▸ Two counters side by side: the sum of the parts, and the actual output. The gap is the drawing.

---

## §2 · The one new hire

**[15]** The clinic has just been given enough money to hire exactly one more person.
▸ A single small figure drawn standing outside the clinic, waiting to be placed.

**[10]** Put them on the front desk, where the queue is.
▸ The figure moves to check-in. Check-in's capacity rises to `75`. The counter: **20**.

**[8]** Put them on triage. Put them on pharmacy.
▸ The figure jumps to triage, then pharmacy. Both bars grow. The counter: **20**. **20**.

**[12]** There is exactly one place that changes the number, and it is not the queue.
▸ The figure lands beside the doctor. The counter finally moves: **20 → 28**.

**[24]** Hire at the front desk and check-in gets faster — so patients reach the waiting room sooner. You have spent the whole budget to make people frustrated sitting down instead of standing up.
▸ The queue outside shortens; the waiting room inside fills to exactly the same total. Same number of figures, rearranged.

**[16]** The receptionist was never the constraint. She was busy absorbing the anger that something else was generating.
▸ The desk drawn small; a long ink line traces the anger back from the desk to the doctor's door.

---

## §3 · The chain

**[13]** The reason this happens has nothing to do with clinics. Picture a chain.
▸ A hand-drawn chain across the screen, links of visibly uneven thickness.

**[8]** Pull harder and harder. Eventually it breaks.
▸ A load gauge, drawn as a scratchy dial, climbs as you scroll. One link reddens. **Snaps at 4.2 tonnes.**

**[7]** How many weakest links does a chain have?
▸ The broken chain hangs. All links grey except one.

**[3]** **One.**
▸ Single link, alone, centred, large.

**[19]** Which means the strength of the whole chain — dozens of links, tons of steel — is set by that single link.
▸ The full chain redrawn; every link fades except the weakest, which stays inked.

**[12]** So reinforce a different link. Then another. Then a third.
▸ Three other links visibly thicken, drawn heavier and darker.

**[9]** Now pull again.
▸ The load gauge climbs. **Snaps at 4.2 tonnes.** Same link. Same number, drawn large.

**[14]** All that improvement, and zero improvement. This is the whole idea, and most of us refuse it.
▸ The two gauge readings side by side, identical.

---

## §4 · Everybody's busy

**[17]** Here is the question that decides whether you have really understood it. Everyone in an organisation is working flat out, all day.
▸ Blank paper. Just the words, in display serif — the only near-full screen of type in the piece.

**[7]** Is that organisation running at its best?
▸ Same, held for a full screen. Nothing else on it.

**[24]** *"If all parts are performing as well as they can, the system as a whole will not be."* — H. William Dettmer
▸ The quotation set large. Behind it, faintly, the five stations all glowing at full tilt.

**[19]** Not "there is room to improve." Everyone working flat out is **positive evidence that your system is running badly.**
▸ The five stations at 100%. The pile in front of the doctor grows and grows and grows as you scroll.

**[21]** A station that isn't the constraint, running at maximum, produces work that the constraint cannot absorb. That work has to go somewhere.
▸ In a factory: inventory stacking. In the clinic: the waiting room filling. Two drawings, same shape.

**[18]** So a well-run system needs most of its parts to have slack — deliberately running below their maximum.
▸ The five stations redrawn: four at half tilt, drawn calm and airy; the doctor at full. No pile.

**[24]** Which produces a fact almost no organisation can act on: picture a dozen energetic improvement teams, each working on something everyone agrees needs fixing.
▸ Twelve small ink squares appear one by one as you scroll.

**[11]** At most one of them is working on the constraint.
▸ One square inks in. The other eleven stay grey — and stay grey, held for a long screen. No animation.

---

## §5 · Don't spend the money yet

**[13]** Back to the clinic. We know it's the doctor. So hire another doctor.
▸ A second doctor figure, drawn faintly, with a price tag.

**[4]** **Not yet.**
▸ The second doctor fades out.

**[21]** Watch her day first. Two hours of it goes on things that do not require a medical degree.
▸ Her day drawn as a bar of hours; two hours shaded a different colour — re-taking histories, hunting notes, printing forms.

**[13]** Take those two hours off her desk. Twenty patients a day becomes twenty-eight.
▸ The counter climbs **20 → 28**. A price tag appears beside it reading **£0**.

**[19]** The next step is harder, because it looks like failure. Everyone else now runs at whatever pace keeps her flat out.
▸ Reception drawn deliberately slowing — the figure standing idle, and it looks *wrong*.

**[22]** Every local metric you have will show those people getting worse, and the clinic getting better. If you can't tolerate that on a dashboard, none of this is available to you.
▸ Four small dials all dropping; one big dial rising.

**[17]** Only now do you spend money. And when you do, you spend it on the doctor, not the loudest queue.
▸ The second doctor returns, placed beside the first.

---

## §6 · The same shape, three more times

**[19]** Ten thousand people visit a website this month. Forty sign up. Three people work on the funnel.
▸ The funnel drawn in exactly the clinic's visual grammar — same ink, same box shapes.

**[18]** If you are improving awareness, conversion and retention equally, you already know you are being inefficient.
▸ Three even effort bars. One stage of the funnel is visibly pinched.

**[14]** The constraint is conversion. Until it moves, another ten thousand visitors is worth nothing.
▸ Traffic doubles at the top; the number at the bottom does not move.

**[16]** A team where every calendar is full and nothing ships. Everything is queued behind the one person who reviews.
▸ A week drawn as five columns, all dense. One desk with a pile in front of it.

**[19]** A parent getting older. A dozen worries, all real. The thing holding everything up is one phone call nobody has made.
▸ A list of a dozen scribbled worries. Eleven fade. One stays inked. *(Softest drawing in the piece.)*

**[14]** The question is never *how hard are we pushing?* It is: **are we pushing on the thing that's stuck?**
▸ All four systems — clinic, funnel, week, list — drawn together at small scale, identical shape.

---

## §7 · It moves

**[12]** Fix the doctor and the clinic does not become fixed.
▸ The five stations. The doctor's bar grows past pharmacy.

**[9]** The constraint moves to the pharmacy.
▸ The inked marker jumps to pharmacy. Counter climbs, then flattens.

**[8]** Fix that, and it moves to follow-up booking.
▸ Marker jumps again. Climbs, flattens.

**[18]** Push far enough and every station inside the clinic outruns the number of people it serves.
▸ All five bars tall. The pile is gone. Counter climbing.

**[16]** So the constraint is gone? It has moved outside the walls — to the hospital that won't take referrals faster.
▸ The view pulls back. The clinic is now one small box; the marker sits on a larger building beyond it.

**[20]** Which is why this never finishes — and why the most dangerous sentence in the whole method is *"we solved that one."*
▸ The wider system drawn, with the marker somewhere new again.

---

## §8 · Everything so far has been cheating

**[16]** You may have noticed this was easy. Five boxes, five numbers, find the smallest.
▸ The five numbered stations, drawn plainly, almost like an exercise.

**[15]** Real organisations are not like that. The constraint is usually not a person or a machine.
▸ The numbers dissolve off the boxes, leaving them blank.

**[3]** **It's a rule.**
▸ Blank paper. Three words.

**[24]** *Every prescription needs the doctor's countersignature.* *No appointment longer than ten minutes.* *Nothing ships without sign-off.* *We don't discuss money in this family.*
▸ Four rules hand-lettered as if pinned to a wall — a noticeboard of curling paper.

**[21]** Every one was installed for a good reason, usually to solve a problem that nobody now remembers.
▸ The noticeboard drawn thicker, layered — old notes under new ones, like sediment.

**[19]** John Gardner called an established organisation *"a coral reef of procedures laid down to achieve some long-forgotten objective."*
▸ The layered notes drawn as an actual reef — accreted, calcified, beautiful and immovable.

**[15]** Breaking one is nearly free, and usually improves things more than buying anything ever would.
▸ A rule torn off the board. Behind it, the counter jumps.

**[22]** And they are invisible, because everyone experiences them as normal. A bottleneck machine has a pile in front of it. A rule has no pile.
▸ Side by side: a machine with a visible pile; a rule with clean empty floor around it.

---

## §9 · So how would you find one?

**[14]** Find the constraint. Put nearly everything into it. Ignore, for now, almost everything else.
▸ The instruction inked plainly, like a sign.

**[26]** But if it is usually a rule — invisible, normal-seeming, defended in good faith, connected to the symptoms you *can* see by long chains that cross every boundary on the org chart —
▸ Faint tangled lines spreading across the whole page, crossing drawn departmental borders.

**[12]** — then how, exactly, would you find one?
▸ The tangle, held. No answer offered.

**[18]** Ask five colleagues what the most limiting thing about your organisation is. You will get five confident, different answers.
▸ Five figures, five speech marks, five different parts of the drawing highlighted.

**[24]** On what basis do you decide who is right? Seniority? Confidence? Volume? Most organisations settle it with authority, anecdote, and whoever tells the best story.
▸ The five answers drawn as competing scribbles, overlapping into illegibility.

**[9]** Which is to say they don't settle it. **They guess.**
▸ The scribbles resolve into one arbitrary mark.

**[21]** Then they spend a year, a budget, and a good deal of everyone's goodwill finding out whether the guess was right.
▸ A calendar year drawn as twelve boxes, filling with ink.

**[19]** That is not a management problem. It is a thinking problem — and nobody ever taught us the technique.
▸ The tangle again, faint, waiting.

## Notes

- **§8 was the risk** — "invisible rules" resist drawing. Solved with the noticeboard →
  sediment → coral reef progression, which turns an abstraction into a physical object that
  accretes. This is the piece's one genuinely new drawing idea; if it fails in sketch, §8
  needs rethinking rather than patching.
- **Scroll-as-Scale** is used once, properly: the pile growing in front of the doctor
  through §4. Everything else would be padding.
- **The Comparison Ladder** is thin here — this subject has no huge quantities. The nearest
  equivalents are `85 vs 20` (§1) and the twelve teams (§4). Don't force more.
- Cold Open deliberately mirrors the reference's shape: attractive setup in good faith, flat
  four-word demolition.
