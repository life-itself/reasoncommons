# Five Shapes — script v1

**Stage 1 of 3.** Pure narrative. No visual direction. Judged as writing only.

Answers: what the Logical Thinking Process actually is.
This is where the method is finally named.

Sources: Dettmer ch3 (IO Map), ch4 (CRT), ch5 (Evaporating Cloud), ch6 (FRT), ch7 (PRT and
the retirement of the Transition Tree).

~1,200 words.

---

The clinic now has something it didn't have: a map of why it is stuck, that five people
built and none of them could break.

It says the ten-minute appointment rule is doing most of the damage. Ten minutes was
introduced to see more people. It means histories get re-taken, notes go unwritten, and a
third of patients come back a second time for the same complaint — which consumes the
appointments the rule was created to protect.

Good. Now what?

Because *why are we stuck* is only one of the questions you need answered, and it isn't even
the first one.

---

Here is the first one, and skipping it is the most expensive mistake in this whole business.

**What should be happening?**

Not what's wrong — what does success actually look like, and what must be true for it to
happen at all? Dettmer calls the failure to ask this the Nero syndrome: fiddling while Rome
burns. Rearranging deck chairs with great diligence.

Because until you have written down what the clinic is *for*, "the problem" is whatever
irritates whoever is talking. Every person measures the place against a private, unstated
idea of what it should be, and then argues about the gap. You cannot have a useful argument
about a gap when nobody has agreed where the far side is.

So: the goal. Then the three or four things that are non-negotiable for reaching it — not
things you'd like, things without which it simply cannot happen. Then what those rest on.

It is an unglamorous document. It is also the ruler. Nothing downstream means anything
without it.

---

**Why isn't it happening?**

Now the symptoms. Waits too long. Staff exhausted. Same patients returning. Referrals late.

The instinct is to fix these, one by one, and that instinct is the whole trap. They aren't
four problems. They are four fingerprints, and if you treat the fingerprints you will be
back here in a year with the same four.

So instead you work backwards. What would have to be true for waits to be this long? And
what would have to be true for *that*? Keep going, and the four symptoms start converging on
a much smaller number of causes — often one or two, sitting somewhere nobody was looking.

That convergence is the point. If a dozen complaints trace back to two causes, then most of
what's wrong is fixable with two changes rather than a dozen. The tangle was never as big as
it looked.

---

**So why hasn't somebody fixed it?**

This question is doing more work than it appears to.

The ten-minute rule has been unpopular for years. Everyone can see the damage. Nobody has
changed it. When a problem is well known, obviously harmful, and *still there*, that is
information: something is holding it in place.

Usually a conflict. Not a personality clash — a structural one, where the thing hurting you
is protecting something else you also need.

Draw it out. The clinic needs to see enough people to serve its population, and it needs
each consultation to be good enough that people don't come back. Ten-minute slots serve the
first. Longer slots serve the second. Both requirements are real. Both actions are
reasonable. They point in opposite directions, and so nothing moves.

Then comes the move that makes this tool worth the trouble. You do not pick a side. You ask
what has to be true for these two to be genuinely opposed — and you write those assumptions
down.

*That consultation length is the only lever on quality.* Is that true? Or does a
consultation overrun because the history was taken twice and the notes weren't there — in
which case the fix isn't longer appointments, it's the notes.

Break one assumption and the conflict doesn't get resolved. It stops existing. That's why
Goldratt called it an evaporating cloud.

---

**Will it actually work?**

You have an idea. Before you spend a year on it, walk it forwards.

If we do this, then what? And if that, then what? Follow it until it either arrives at the
thing you wanted or doesn't. This is where a surprising number of good ideas quietly die,
on paper, for free.

And then the question people skip because they don't want the answer: **what else does it
do?** Every change enters a system of interdependencies, and the system talks back. The
fixes in this story all worked on the thing they targeted and broke something adjacent.

You are looking for those breakages now, while they are hypothetical and cost nothing.

---

**What do we do on Monday, and in what order?**

Ideas are not solutions. An idea becomes a solution when it is implemented and doing what it
was supposed to do, and the road between those two states is where most good ideas are
buried.

So: what stands in the way? List the obstacles honestly — money, skills, permissions,
sequence, and the fact that a change obvious to you is a threat to someone whose numbers
currently look fine. Then, against each obstacle, the thing that would have to exist to get
past it. Then put them in the only order they can happen in.

Not a plan of tasks. A plan of obstacles.

---

Five questions. Five differently shaped answers.

**What should be happening** — a hierarchy of necessity: the goal, and what it stands on.
**Why isn't it** — a web of cause and effect, symptoms at the top, roots at the bottom.
**What holds it in place** — a small diagram of a conflict, and the assumptions underneath.
**Will it work** — the same cause-and-effect logic, run forwards.
**What do we do** — obstacles, answers, sequence.

Together they are called the Logical Thinking Process. Goldratt built them; Dettmer spent
decades teaching and refining them.

That's it. That's the method. It has a reputation for being forbidding, and it is really
five questions asked in a particular order, with the arrows checked.

---

There were six.

The sixth was a step-by-step implementation map, and in the 2007 edition Dettmer largely
removed it — writing that over ten years of teaching he had found it "the least valuable of
the Thinking Process tools for nearly all practitioners."

That is worth pausing on. The person who spent his career on this method looked at his own
toolkit and took a tool out of it.

You should trust the remaining five more, not less, for that.

---

One last thing, and it is the thing that actually matters.

The diagrams are not the product. Nobody needs more diagrams.

What they do is convert reasoning from something happening privately inside a head into an
object sitting on a table — where it can be read, tested, disagreed with in a specific
place, and repaired. The tree is to thinking what written notation is to music: not the
performance, but the thing that lets the performance be shared, criticised and improved by
people who weren't in the room.

Which produces the property that makes any of this worth the effort. **Claims get
addresses.** A vague unease about strategy becomes an objection to a specific node, which
can be found, argued with, and fixed. Disagreement stops being weather and starts being
work. And the map keeps what the argument concluded, so the same discussion doesn't have to
be held again in March, warmly, from the beginning.

That is the actual promise. Not better diagrams. A group whose thinking accumulates.

---

Back at the clinic, someone has just noticed that if you fix the notes, the ten-minute rule
might not need changing at all.

Somebody else thinks that's wrong.

For the first time, they can find out.
