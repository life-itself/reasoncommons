# Critique — B4, *Why nobody does the filing*

Adversarial read of `draft.md`, 2026-09-20, against `voice-guide.md` (items 6, 9 and 11 held hardest), `04-arc-v2.md` ("The reader", "What they should be able to say afterwards", Series B, and A7), `01-evaluation.md` (findings 4 and 5), and the approved `b3-give-every-claim-an-address/script.md`. Prose only; illustrations not judged and none proposed.

Verdict up front: the dilemma work in the middle of this chapter is the best thing in Series B so far. "Nobody joined a movement for civilizational renewal in order to fill in forms" and "The people already inside don't do it either" together do, in forty words, what the published piece needed a conflict diagram and a page of ceremony to fail at — the shared objective is named, both horns are real, and nobody is blamed. Three things spoil it. **First, evaluation finding 5 is reintroduced essentially unchanged**: the AI turn is one paragraph of assertion about "current models", with no instance, no provenance for its own hedges, and — worse than the published piece — no forward pointer to the chapter that will show it. Second, **the chapter bolds the sentence it is about to demolish**, so a phone reader who takes only the bold takes away the opposite of the chapter's message; the arc is right and the draft is wrong. Third, **the handover from B3 is mis-stated**: B3's entire closing move is that *we* placed Maren's sentence and the reading is ours and might be wrong, and B4 opens by saying the last chapter asked *Maren* to do it. It did not. That error is not cosmetic — it puts the cost on the wrong person and lets the chapter declare a dilemma dissolved that has in fact moved to the maintainer, where the chapter never looks.

Body word count: **521** (lines 11–31, excluding the header block and the key-claim line). In band, ~79 words of headroom — enough for the one instance the chapter is missing, if something else goes.

## 1. The restatement test — and *which* assumption breaks

**Key claim:** *Structure takes effort, so it looks like a choice between easy and cumulative — but that rests on one assumption: that the person contributing has to do the filing.*

**Can a cold reader say it back?** The first two clauses, yes, and well. They will come off this chapter able to say *"putting your point in the right place is a lot of work, so it looks like you can have a friendly forum or a record that adds up but not both."* That is transmitted cleanly, with a person in it, and it is the hardest half to write.

**Can they say which assumption breaks?** Here it comes apart, because the draft's bolded assumption is three assumptions in a trench coat: **that the structure has to be put there (a) by a person, (b) specifically by the person contributing, (c) at the moment they contribute.** The key-claim line at the top of the file names only one of them — "that the person contributing has to do the filing" — and that is the right one. The bold in the body names all three, and the chapter's own resolution only breaks two.

Follow the logic. The draft says AI is "not yet well enough to trust with what the group holds", and B5's whole frame (per the arc) is "people decide what goes in". So after the turn, a person still puts the structure there. Assumption (a) is not broken; it is exactly as true as it was. What breaks is (b) and (c): the person is no longer the contributor, and no longer at the moment of contributing. By overreaching into (a), the bold makes a claim the chapter itself refutes four sentences later, and it takes the one thing a reader most needs to be able to repeat — *which* assumption died — and blurs it. A reader asked afterwards "so what was the assumption?" will most likely answer "that a human had to do it", which is the reading the chapter has to spend B5 walking back.

The draft half-knows this, and the sentence where it half-knows it is the best sentence in the chapter: "which meant the contributor, or a maintainer with time nobody has." That clause says the true thing — the alternative to the contributor always existed, and it was unaffordable. **The chapter's own honest assumption is about cost, not about agency**, and the bold should say so: that the reading has to be done by someone who already knows both the sentence and the structure, at the moment of contributing, which meant the contributor or nobody.

## 2. The handover from B3 is mis-stated, in two ways

> Because of what the last chapter quietly asked of Maren. To put her sentence where it belongs she would have had to find the tree, learn what a box and an arrow mean there, read enough of it to know which of the eight conditions her point bears on, phrase her objection in the grammar of necessity, and then defend that placement to the people who drew the thing.

**B3 did not ask this of Maren. B3 did it for her, by hand, and said so twice.** The approved script's last two paragraphs are: *"Read in the tree's grammar, she is proposing a further condition under that node…"* and *"Somebody still has to decide that is what she meant. That reading is ours, not hers, and it could be wrong."* Its source note makes this load-bearing and forbids a build from softening it. So B4's premise is a counterfactual about a chapter the reader did not read: in the world B3 depicted, Maren wrote one sentence and *we* paid the filing cost. The five-step list is a cost that in the story fell on us.

This is not pedantry, because correcting it strengthens the chapter enormously. The true version is better: *somebody had to do that reading, and in the last chapter the somebody was us, by hand, for one sentence.* That (i) is faithful to B3, (ii) makes the cost visible as recurring work rather than as a hypothetical burden on a newcomer who never bore it, (iii) sets up the maintainer as the person who actually can't scale, which is where §5 says the conflict really lives, and (iv) makes the AI turn land on a job the reader has just watched being done by hand — which is the cheapest possible version of the demonstration the chapter lacks.

**Second mis-statement: the opening question answers a set-up that no longer exists.** "If it is this useful, why is almost nobody working this way?" is B3's deleted trailer, resurrected. B3's critique killed *"What it does not yet explain is why, if it is this simple, hardly anybody does it"* (fix 3) and the approved script duly ends on "an argument the tree can now hold". B4 then opens by asking the question B3 no longer asks, in nearly the same words, as though the handoff were still in place. It also asks the chapter's own title back at the reader in rhetorical form, and the published form prints that title above the prose. Twelve words, and all of them are stall.

**Third, a smaller one: B3 ends on an open problem that B4 walks past.** "That reading is ours, not hers, and it could be wrong — which is an argument the tree can now hold." The live question B3 hands forward is *who decides the reading, and what happens when it is wrong.* B4 answers a different question. When the AI turn arrives, the contestability of a placement is the first thing a reader who remembers B3 will reach for, and the chapter never picks it up.

## 3. Finding 5: the turn is asserted, not shown — and the promissory note is not even written

The whole chapter stands on this paragraph:

> That assumption is no longer true in the age of AI. Reading a piece of prose against a structure and proposing where it fits is exactly the shape of thing current models are good at. Not perfectly, not unsupervised, and not yet well enough to trust with what the group holds — but well enough to do the reading, which is the part that was never going to scale.

Compare finding 5 on the published piece: *"The load-bearing claim gets one bold line, and the small print admits matching isn't good enough to run unattended."* That is a description of this paragraph. One assertion, one hedge that concedes the unattended case, nothing shown. Structurally it is the same artefact; the only difference is that the published version's small print was in a footnote and this one is in the same sentence.

Three specific holes:

**No instance.** "Exactly the shape of thing current models are good at" is a claim about a capability, made in the register of common knowledge, to a reader the arc describes as "not a management-science reader". They have no way to evaluate it and no reason to believe it beyond the writer's confidence. A newcomer who is sceptical of AI — a large fraction of this audience, arriving from a movement about civilizational renewal — reads this sentence as the hand-wave where the argument was supposed to be.

**No provenance for the hedges, which is the odd part, because the hedges are the evidence.** "Not perfectly, not unsupervised, and not yet well enough to trust with what the group holds" is knowledge that can only come from having tried it. The draft states it as if from nowhere. Saying *we ran this* costs four words and converts the entire paragraph from opinion into report — and the calibration of the hedge then becomes the thing that makes the confident half credible.

**No forward pointer at all.** The chapter defers its demonstration to B5 and never tells the reader it is doing so. The only forward reference in the chapter points sideways, at A7. So the reader is not given a promissory note; they are given a bare claim and then, in the last line, sent to a different series. That is worse than the published piece, which at least admitted its limits where the reader could see them.

**Is the deferral acceptable?** The deferral, yes — the arc assigns the worked example to B5 and B5 should keep it. The chapter as drafted, no: the dilemma is only dissolved if the AI claim is true, so the argument does collapse without something to stand on. What it needs is not B5's material but the single case the reader is already holding:

1. **One instance, ~40 words.** The placement in B3 — the real tree, Maren's sentence, the node under "small dense groups". Say that we gave a model the tree and the sentence, that it proposed that node, that it offered a second reading (her sentence also reads as an objection to the top claim, which B3's own critique noted), and name one thing it got wrong or could not place. This does not steal B5: B5 is a rehearsal transcript, several contributors, confidences, one contribution the tree can't hold. This is one sentence the reader watched being filed by hand in the previous chapter, now filed by a machine. It is the cheapest demonstration available and it is already written elsewhere in the process material.
2. **One clause of provenance**, so the hedges are findings rather than modesty.
3. **One sentence naming what stays human**, which is also the fix for §4 and §5: the proposal is not the decision.

With those three, the chapter stands alone. Without them, its load-bearing paragraph is an assertion the series has now made twice in two forms, and the evaluation will be able to copy finding 5 forward unedited.

## 4. The hedging: overclaim and undercut inside one paragraph

The paragraph's shape is advance → triple retreat → re-advance, in three sentences. "No longer true in the age of AI" (flat, absolute, no scope). "Exactly the shape of thing current models are good at" (confident, unevidenced, "exactly" doing the work an example should do). Then "not perfectly, not unsupervised, and not yet well enough to trust with what the group holds". Then "but well enough to do the reading, which is the part that was never going to scale".

**The middle retreat concedes the thing the series exists for.** What the group holds is the subject of B1 ("what a group agrees, it loses"), B2 and B3. A hedge that says the enabler cannot yet be trusted with *what the group holds* is, read literally, a hedge that says the enabler cannot yet do the job. The resolution — it proposes, people decide, and deciding on a proposal is a fraction of the work of producing one — is what makes the hedge coherent, and the chapter does not say it. So the honesty reads as self-cancellation rather than as calibration. The fix is not a softer hedge; it is the missing sentence that makes the hedge survivable.

**"Which is the part that was never going to scale" is an assertion smuggled in as a summary.** Is reading the part that doesn't scale, or is deciding? The chapter has just told us a maintainer would have to do both and that nobody has time for either. Asserting that one half was the bottleneck — with no argument — is the chapter quietly picking the answer that suits it. If it is true, one clause of why: a placement takes a maintainer twenty minutes to work out and ten seconds to approve.

**And one overclaim elsewhere:** "Until very recently the only thing that could do that was a person who knew both the sentence and the structure." Not true, and B2 already supplies the true version: machines have filed text for decades, by keyword, topic and tag — which, in B2's own words, is filing "by where a claim was written down rather than by what it asserts". The honest and much stronger sentence is that machines could always file by *topic* and never by *assertion*, and assertion is the thing an address needs. That reuses a line the reader has met, is checkable, and is the actual news.

## 5. Does the dilemma dissolve, or does it move?

> The dilemma dissolves rather than being settled. Contribution stays as easy as posting on a forum, and the filing still gets done, by something that does not mind doing it.

**It dissolves for the contributor and relocates onto the maintainer, and the chapter does not look there.** Take the chapter's own commitments seriously: AI proposes, it is not trusted unsupervised, people decide what goes in. Then every contribution generates a proposed placement that a human must accept, reject or re-read. Contribution has been made cheaper, which by the chapter's own argument means there will be more of it, which means more proposals to review. The reviewing load scales with the thing that was just made frictionless. That is not a fatal objection — a yes/no on a proposal is genuinely far cheaper than producing one, and reviewing can be batched, shared, and done by the people who already care — but it is the objection, and the chapter's silence about it is what makes "by something that does not mind doing it" read as a sleight of hand.

Two further places the conflict shows up, both of which the chapter has already been handed:

- **"Defend that placement to the people who drew the thing"** is item five on the draft's own cost list, and nothing in the resolution removes it. This is finding 5's second sentence quoted almost verbatim — *"'Defend it' is on the stairs and AI doesn't remove it"* — reproduced inside the new chapter. Either drop it from the list (it is arguably not a filing cost at all, it is the cost of being disagreed with, which is the thing B3 says the tree makes *cheap*) or answer it: what AI removes is the cost of *finding the place*; defending a placement is now an argument about one arrow, which B3 has already shown is small.
- **Who decides the reading was right?** B3's last paragraph. If the machine's proposal is the reading, and the reading can be wrong, then the contestability moves from "Maren must phrase it correctly" to "somebody must check the machine did". That is a better trade, and saying so is a stronger dissolution than claiming the cost vanished.

**And the series' own companion material supplies the vocabulary for honesty here.** A2's key claim is *"expect it to move once you do"* — squeeze a constraint and it relocates. B4 declares a constraint eliminated. A reader who does A after B will notice the inconsistency; a reader who does B after A will notice it immediately. One clause — *the work does not vanish, it moves to a much smaller job and to the people who were going to do it anyway* — costs nothing and is both truer and more in the series' voice.

**A separate soft spot in the cloud's construction.** Horn A is stated as an outcome, not a need: "Keep contributing easy … and accept that the good parts dissolve into the scroll." A cloud only dissolves convincingly if the reader believes *both* sides are necessary. The chapter says why structure is needed (it cumulates); it never says why ease is needed beyond "people won't pay". Ease is a need in its own right — a forum where people say what they actually think is where the good sentences come from in the first place — and voice item 11 asks for exactly that framing. See §6.

**One more thing the chapter asserts without checking, in a series about checking.** "Why is almost nobody working this way?" is answered with one cause: effort. There are others — people disagree about what the tree should say, trees get captured by whoever maintains them, the tooling has never been usable. A3 in the companion series is *about* five sensible people giving five different causes. A single-cause answer, unhedged, in this series, is a small self-inflicted wound. One clause fixes it: effort is not the only reason, but it is the one that has just changed.

## 6. Maren, and the newcomer's side of the cost (voice item 11)

> Every one of those is reasonable. Together they are a morning's work before she has said anything. She is twenty-three, she has a dissertation, and she came to the forum because there was a conversation happening. She is not going to do the filing, and it would be strange of us to expect it.

**Mostly generous, and one word tips it.** "It would be strange of us to expect it" is the target register exactly — "we", the cost owned by us rather than charged to her. "A morning's work before she has said anything" is concrete and fair. The evaluation praised "She has a dissertation to write" as exactly right, and that half survives.

**"She is twenty-three" is the problem, and it is a problem of argument, not of taste.** The dissertation is a *reason* — a competing claim on the same hours. An age is not a reason; it is a characterisation, and the implicit inference the reader draws is that not-filing is a thing young people do. That is precisely the condescension item 11 warns against, and the draft disproves it itself in the very next paragraph: "The people already inside don't do it either." If nobody does it, her age explains nothing and reads as an excuse made on her behalf by someone older. Cut three words and the paragraph gets stronger.

**The positive half of item 11 is missing.** The guide asks for the cost of structure to newcomers *and* for "accessibility, ease and joy as goals, not obstacles". The draft treats ease entirely as a constraint to be worked around: people won't pay the entry price, therefore don't charge it. Nowhere does it say that an easy forum is worth having on its own terms — that unstructured talk is where the good sentences come from, and Maren's sentence, which the whole series hangs on, is a thing she produced *because* nobody asked her to file it. B2 had the generous version and it is one line: "What a newcomer is best placed to give is the thing we are worst at receiving." B4 is the chapter that most needs that line and does not have it.

**"Nobody joined a movement for civilizational renewal in order to fill in forms" is the best sentence in the chapter** and does more for item 11 than anything else on the page — warm, light (item 10), and it puts the writer on the same side as the reader. Protect it.

## 7. The bolded sentence is the wrong one — the arc is right

The draft bolds the assumption. The arc specifies **"no longer true in the age of AI."** The arc wins, on four grounds.

**The bold is the thing the chapter destroys.** Four paragraphs after the bold, the chapter says the bolded sentence is false. On a phone, in this form, the bolded line is what a skimmer carries away. A reader who reads the bold and nothing else leaves believing that structure has to be added by the contributor at the moment of contributing — the exact belief the chapter exists to remove. No amount of surrounding prose fixes an emphasis that is pointed at the premise.

**It breaks the pattern the series has established.** B1 bolds the diagnosis it has just proved. B2 bolds the consequence. B3 bolds the payoff ("Her proposal has an address before anyone has agreed with it"). In every case the bold is the chapter's conclusion, and it sits late. B4's bold sits at the midpoint and is a setup.

**Voice item 6 quotes the target sentence verbatim** as the model of the rewrite, and the item is titled "Name AI as the enabler". Bolding the assumption does the opposite: it gives the visual weight to the status quo and leaves the enabler in ordinary type.

**But the turn cannot simply be bolded as written**, because §1 and §4 show it is currently overstated (it negates an assumption broader than the one that actually breaks) and unevidenced. Fix the assumption's wording first so the turn is its exact negation, then bold the turn. Keep the assumption plain or italic — it does not need help; it is a sentence nobody disputes.

## 8. Findings 4 and 7 — partial reintroductions

**Finding 4 ("the catch and the dilemma make one point twice"): reintroduced in prose, though not as ceremony.** The arc's instruction — "the dilemma in words, no cloud diagram" — is followed, and that is a real win: there is no diagram, no five-box apparatus, no restatement of the conflict as a figure. But the *cost* point is now made four times in four consecutive paragraphs: the five-step list (para 2), Maren's morning and her dissertation (para 3), nobody inside does it either (para 4), and then again as horn one of the dilemma (para 5, "most people will not pay the entry price"). Paragraphs 2–4 are 175 words establishing one proposition — filing is expensive — which the reader accepted at the end of paragraph 2. That is the same duplication the evaluation caught, redistributed. Paragraph 4 is the one that earns its place (it is the surprising one: the insiders don't do it either), paragraph 2 is the concrete one. Paragraph 3 could lose half its length, and horn one of the dilemma could stop re-arguing a settled point and instead say why ease is *wanted*.

**There is also a repetition across chapters the draft has not noticed.** The five-step list is structurally identical to B2's — *"To act on her objection she would have had to find the paper, find the page, work out whether the sentence she was arguing with was load-bearing or throat-clearing, then reconstruct all of that for everyone else."* Same character, same "she would have had to" construction, same escalating list, same conclusion. As written it reads as a repeat. Named, it becomes the chapter's sharpest move: **the tree we just sold you has the same disease we diagnosed in the papers** — we replaced one filing cost with another. That is a genuinely adversarial observation about the series' own proposal, it is true, and it makes the dilemma inevitable rather than announced. The draft is one sentence away from it.

**Finding 7 ("undefined terms: 'reason commons', 'cumulate'"): half-reintroduced.** "Reason commons" is correctly absent. But "so it cumulates" is there in paragraph 5 — the flagged word, unglossed — and the plain synonym the series already uses is in the *next paragraph*: "a group whose thinking actually compounds". B1 taught "compounds" and built a whole paragraph on it. Use it in both places and the flagged term disappears at no cost. (The key-claim line's "cumulative" is fine as an internal label but should not surface in the prose.)

## 9. Sentences doing no work, or existing to sound good

> If it is this useful, why is almost nobody working this way?

**The opening, and the clearest cut in the chapter.** Three faults, covered in §2: it revives a trailer B3 deleted; it asks the chapter's printed title back at the reader; and "this useful" attributes to B3 a claim about usefulness B3 did not make (B3 claimed that disagreement becomes cheap). It is also the series' first rhetorical question used as an opener, where B1 opened on a person, B2 on a flat statement and B3 on a list of properties. Open instead on the concrete thing: the reading somebody did by hand in the last chapter.

> The dilemma dissolves rather than being settled.

**Grading the argument instead of making it** — the same family as B3's "That is the whole grammar", which its critique cut. The reader can see whether it dissolved; telling them it did is the writer marking their own work, and it invites the scepticism §5 says is warranted. Cut the sentence; keep the one after it.

> Say it out loud and you can see it is not a law of nature.

**Two writerly gestures in one sentence.** "Say it out loud" is a stage direction, "not a law of nature" is a flourish that says only *this is contingent*. The next sentence — "It is a description of what used to be affordable" — says the same thing concretely and is the best nine words on the page. Delete the first and let the second land.

> by something that does not mind doing it

**Cute, and it is the overclaim in miniature.** It implies the filing is now fully done by the machine, which the paragraph above denies. It is also the chapter's only anthropomorphism and the closest thing to an aphoristic close, which is what voice item 1 exists to cut.

> This move — find the shared objective, find the assumption underneath the conflict, break the assumption — is itself a tool from the method, and there is a chapter about it in the companion series.

**Admin, vague, and in the wrong place** — see §10.

> the research circle in chapter one

**A register break, small but new to the series.** Combined with "the last chapter" in paragraph 2, this is the first B chapter to refer to itself as a sequence of chapters in the prose. B2 carried continuity with "So the record is not the problem" and B3 with "Earlier this year we built one"; neither ever said "chapter". Say "the research circle", or "the circle that keeps re-deciding the same thing" — the reader who needs the reminder gets it, and the reader who doesn't isn't handed a table of contents.

> phrase her objection in the grammar of necessity

**New jargon coined at the moment it is charged as a cost.** B3 taught *in order to have this, we need that* and never used the phrase "grammar of necessity". Using the taught words here is plainer and does double duty: it demonstrates the cost rather than naming it, because the reader feels the awkwardness of having to recast their own sentence into somebody else's form.

## 10. The last line — ending or trailer?

**Trailer, and admin, and the wrong destination.** Three problems stacked.

It points at "a chapter about it in the companion series" when the arc is specific: **A7, *Why hasn't somebody fixed it? The cloud*** — *"A problem persists when fixing it seems to cost something else we need; name the assumption behind that conflict and it can often be dissolved."* B3's critique made the identical finding about "the companion series" (fix 11) and the approved script duly named A5 and A4. B4 regresses.

It credits "the method" without naming anything, where item 5 asks for lineage named and B3 set the precedent by naming the Logical Thinking Process outright. If the line stays, it should name what this move is — the evaporating cloud — which is both the credit and the useful thing for a reader who wants to look it up.

And it ends the chapter on an errand. The last thing a reader is told before the chapter that *pays this one off* is: go and read a different series about a technique. Meanwhile B5 — the chapter that supplies the missing demonstration — is not mentioned anywhere. If any forward pointer belongs here it is that one, and per §3 it is close to obligatory.

**There is no ending in the draft at all**, which is the deeper problem: the chapter stops. B1 ends on the record being complete and the movement still not remembering. B2 ends on the statute. B3 ends on the argument the tree can now hold. B4 ends on a cross-reference. Two candidate endings are already in the draft. The stronger one is a corrected version of "Contribution stays as easy as posting on a forum" — because it closes the loop on Maren and the dilemma in one move, and because the true, modest version is more striking than the overclaim: *Maren writes her sentence the way she wrote it. Nothing is asked of her that was not asked before.* The other is "It is a description of what used to be affordable", which is the intellectual close. Either ends on something; the current line ends on a footnote.

## 11. The voice-guide checklist, box by box

- [~] **Could a reader who has never heard of LTP say, after this chapter, the one or two things it set out to say?** Half. They can say the dilemma, in their own words, with a person in it — that part is well made. They cannot say *which* assumption broke, because the bolded assumption bundles three and the turn only breaks two (§1); and what they will most likely repeat — "AI does the filing now" — is stronger than what the chapter actually claims (§4). A chapter that transmits a stronger claim than it makes has a calibration problem, not a clarity problem.
- [ ] **Is each concept stated plainly before (or as) it is illustrated?** The dilemma: stated plainly, illustrated with Maren and the research circle. Pass. The assumption: stated plainly. Pass. **The AI capability — the load-bearing one — is stated plainly and never illustrated at all** (§3). The chapter's three concepts score two out of three, and it fails on the one the argument rests on.
- [ ] **Any sentence whose job is to sound good rather than to say something?** At least five, quoted in §9: the opening question; "The dilemma dissolves rather than being settled"; "Say it out loud and you can see it is not a law of nature"; "by something that does not mind doing it"; and the closing cross-reference, which is admin rather than flourish but is equally cuttable.
- [~] **"We" voice; lineage credited where a method is used; AI named where it is the enabler.** Mixed. **AI is named, in the right place, in the arc's own words — pass, and it is the reason the chapter exists.** But the "we" is thin: one instance ("it would be strange of us to expect it") against second-person drift in exactly the paragraphs where the dilemma is ours — "the newcomers you most want", "when two things you want look incompatible". This is our dilemma and the movement's; say so. **Lineage: a miss.** The cloud move is described in three steps and attributed to "the method" and "a chapter in the companion series", with nothing named. B3 named the Logical Thinking Process; this chapter borrows the evaporating cloud and credits nobody.
- [~] **One bolded key claim at most per section.** Exactly one bold, so numerically compliant — but it is on the premise rather than the claim, which is the emphasis error §7 argues is the second most damaging thing in the draft.
- [ ] **Every hedge honest, no overclaim, the opposing view stated fairly.** Fails on all three legs. Overclaim: "the only thing that could do that was a person" (machines have filed by topic for decades — B2's own distinction fixes it); "the filing still gets done, by something that does not mind doing it" (contradicted by the preceding paragraph). Hedge honesty: the triple hedge concedes the series' own objective and is never reconciled (§4), and it floats without the provenance that would license it. Opposing view: **absent.** Not one objection to the AI turn is voiced — not "it will file things wrong and you will get a tree of plausible garbage nobody trusts", not "who checks the machine", not "reviewing every proposal is its own job". §5 shows the chapter had two of these handed to it by its own previous chapter and by A2.
- [x] **300–600 words.** 521. In band, ~79 spare. Fix 1 needs about 45 of them and §8's compression frees more.

## 12. What is working — protect this in revision

- **"Nobody joined a movement for civilizational renewal in order to fill in forms."** The best line in the chapter: warm, light, true, and it does item 11's work in twelve words without a trace of condescension. It also redeems "civilizational renewal", which the evaluation flagged as manifesto language, by using it ironically and late rather than earnestly and first.
- **"The people already inside don't do it either: the research circle in chapter one takes minutes, which is the cheap version, and even that produced nothing the movement holds."** The chapter's strongest argument and its most surprising turn. It kills the lazy-newcomer reading before the reader can form it, it converts an individual failing into a structural one, and — unlike the B3 cross-reference — it is factually faithful to B1 ("The circle takes minutes") and B2 ("Nothing the movement holds was any different"). Any rewrite must keep this paragraph's job.
- **"It is a description of what used to be affordable."** Nine words, and the whole turn is in them. It frames the assumption as an economic fact rather than a conceptual error, which is both true and the only framing under which AI is a plausible answer. If one sentence survives untouched, this one.
- **"which meant the contributor, or a maintainer with time nobody has."** Honest, and it names the real constraint. It is also the hook for every fix in §5 and §2 — do not lose it in a compression pass.
- **"Both sides of that want the same thing: a group whose thinking actually compounds."** The correct cloud move in plain words: the shared objective, named before the assumption is hunted. Item 9 asks for exactly this and the draft delivers it without a diagram, which is what finding 4 was complaining about. The dilemma-in-prose is a clear improvement on the published piece.
- **The five-step cost list.** Concrete, each step real, each step checkable against B3. Its attribution is wrong (§2) but its content is the best evidence in the chapter.
