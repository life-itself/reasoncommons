<p class="ch-lede">A tree of claims gives every claim, and every link between them, a place you can point to — so Maren's suggestion has somewhere to land.</p>

What if the thing a group held in common was not a pile of documents and threads, but a structure underneath them: a tree?

A tree helps you structure a thesis. Its root — the foundational node — is the top claim. You break that into the sub-claims it depends on, and those into theirs, as far down as is useful. Every claim then has a location, and so does every link between claims, because a link is a claim too: *in order to have this, we need that*. What you are looking at is not only the conclusions but the reasoning underneath them.

Earlier this year we did that for the Second Renaissance, drawing the tree out of the movement's own written corpus. It says that several things have to be true at once for a Second Renaissance to be more than an idea: people understanding the crises as one crisis, small dense groups making the new way of living livable, and six more.

Now her suggestion has somewhere to go. What she is saying is that the tree is missing a condition: small, dense groups only make a new way of living livable if they are rooted in a place somebody actually lives in. That is a proposed new node, hanging under one of the eight, and anyone can see exactly what it would change.

**Her disagreement gets sharper as well as more usable.** She doesn't have to reject the whole strategy in order to say something; she only has to argue that one condition is missing. And when someone answers her, they are answering *that*, at an address both of them can return to in a year.

This is a method, not our invention. It comes out of the Logical Thinking Process, and we say more about how the trees are built, and how you check an arrow, in the companion series.
