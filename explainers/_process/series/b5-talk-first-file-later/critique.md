## 1. Fact-check, against `proposals.yaml` and its inputs

This is the chapter whose whole job is to be an evidence claim, so I have checked every assertion about the run. Most of the detail is right — impressively so, and several phrases are near-verbatim from the YAML's own rationales. Four things are not, and the first two are serious.

**A. "Nine people contributed to a session on the Second Renaissance's strategy, in the way people contribute to sessions" — not supported, and the most damaging sentence in the draft.** The YAML's first three lines say the nine contributors are fictional, the input is `talk/2r_participant_contributions_test.docx`, and that this is "rehearsal material, not the room's words." The docx's own title block reads "Demo C test input · fictional participant data." No session produced these paragraphs. The draft's sentence asserts an event that did not happen, in the present-tense reportorial register ("Here is the run"), and it does so while naming a real thing — the Second Renaissance's strategy — which makes the false part specific rather than vague. `meta.event` in the YAML is "Second Renaissance Research Group" and `meta.date` is 2026-09-04, a date on which a real talk did occur; a reader who cross-checks will conclude the draft attached invented people to a real meeting. The correction in the last paragraph ("The contributors in this run were rehearsal, not the room") arrives 460 words later and does not undo it. See also §2.

**B. "in the way people contribute to sessions: a paragraph each, in their own words, nobody naming a node or learning a notation" — half supported, and the half that fails is the chapter's thesis.** "A paragraph each, in their own words, nobody naming a node" is supported: the docx's facilitator note says the notes "have not been translated into LTP terminology, deduplicated, or assigned to nodes in the existing tree," and `VERIFICATION.md` line 100 confirms the nine were "written by someone else, in their own words." But the prompt those contributors were given, printed in the docx above the nine paragraphs, is: *"After looking at the current Second Renaissance reasoning, what would you add, change, question, or challenge? What feels missing or incorrectly assumed?"* They had seen the reasoning. They were asked to respond to it. That is not "talk first, file later" — it is a structured review round. The chapter's title, its key claim ("AI reads the conversation afterwards"), and the line "Afterwards we gave those paragraphs and the movement's real tree to a model" all assert that the reading was done on unprompted conversation. The demo does not show that. It shows the easier case: AI placing contributions that were already aimed at the tree. This is the single strongest objection a hostile reader could make to the whole series, because B1–B4 have spent 2,000 words arguing that the value is in catching what people say when they are *not* thinking about the tree. The draft must either say what the prompt was, or stop claiming the contributions were untargeted. (Note a live contradiction in the repo you are inheriting: `DECK.md` line 121 calls them "written independently of the tree," which the docx's own prompt does not support. Don't propagate it.)

**C. "the model has no category for what a culture is like to belong to" — wrong referent, and it inverts the point.** The draft writes: "So the model declined to place it, and said why: the model has no category for what a culture is like to belong to, as distinct from what it asks of people." In `proposals.yaml` (PROP-009 rationale) "the model" means the *LTP model* — the tree. The draft has just used "the model" four times to mean the AI. So the sentence as written says the AI lacks the category, which is (i) not what the source says, (ii) not true, and (iii) the exact opposite of the sentence that follows it two lines later, "it is the tree telling you where it is thin." The draft refutes itself inside four lines. Fix by never using "the model" for the AI in this chapter (use "the reader", as B4 does) or by writing "the tree has no category."

**D. "some propose a new condition" — incomplete, and it hides that the run spans two trees.** The nine break down as four `challenge_entity`, one `challenge_link`, three `add_entity`, one `unplaced`. Of the three additions, only two (Leila's safeguarding layer under CSF-3, Sofia's useful-work condition under NC-5) are necessary conditions; Marcus's is a `root_cause` added under UDE-3 in the `current-reality` view, not a condition. And seven placements are in `goal-tree`, two in `current-reality` — the YAML header makes a point of the running order being arranged around that switch, and `VERIFICATION.md` records "3 added claims across two views." The draft says "the movement's real tree," singular, throughout. That is a small inaccuracy with a real cost: B2's whole argument is about *addresses*, and "two different trees for two different questions" is a fact about the address space that this chapter is the only place to show. At minimum: "some name a cause the tree was missing" instead of "some propose a new condition," and one clause acknowledging that the placements landed across two of the movement's trees.

Everything else checks out, and I want that on the record because the fixes above should not be done by rewriting the parts that are correct:

- Maya's paraphrase is faithful to PROP-001's `source.text`, including "broad enough to justify almost anything."
- The italicised reading is a near-verbatim lift of `interpretation.statement`, with "G-1" rendered as "the goal". Correct.
- "Not that the goal is wrong — that it cannot rule anything out" matches `proposal.rationale` almost word for word. Correct.
- The alternative reading (intermediate goal under the root, "sustaining a few local communities that live the values over time") is PROP-001's `alternatives[0].note`, quoted accurately.
- Why it ranked lower — "she offers it as an illustration of what is missing rather than as a proposal" — is `interpretation.note`, accurately rendered, and "it says so, rather than choosing silently" is a fair gloss on the alternative being recorded at all.
- "six readings came back high confidence and three medium": correct. I counted them (001 high, 002 medium, 003 high, 004 high, 005 high, 006 medium, 007 high, 008 high, 009 medium) and the YAML header states the same split.
- Ana's paraphrase, the nearest address being the durable-embodiment condition (CSF-1), and "filing it there would have made celebration and grief instrumental, a means to adoption, which is close to the reading Ana was wary of" are all faithful to PROP-009's rationale, the last almost verbatim.
- "Nothing in that run was merged": correct. All nine carry `review.status: pending`.

Three smaller precision notes. (i) "Transform civilization" appears in the draft in quotation marks with no attribution, immediately after "the big civilizational goal," so it reads as the tree's wording of the root. It is not: G-1 reads "Co-initiate a conscious transformation toward a wiser, weller, regenerative civilization." The quote belongs to Maya. Attribute it. (ii) "marked as the less likely one" is your inference from `confidence: high` versus `confidence: medium`; the YAML never ranks them as likelihoods, and these are a model's self-reported labels, not calibrated probabilities. Defensible, but if you keep "less likely," one clause somewhere should say the confidences are the reader's own estimate of itself. (iii) "the movement's real tree" — real in the sense of built from the movement's real documents, yes; but G-1 is `status: provisional`, and the model's own open questions include "Do actual participants confirm G-1 as the operating goal?" Your later hedge ("the trees are provisional and wrong in places") covers this, just; don't weaken that hedge in revision.

And one omission that is a fact-check matter even though nothing in the draft is false about it: `VERIFICATION.md` states plainly, "That is still not evidence the readings are *right* — no one with standing over the tree has reviewed them." The draft hedges the pipeline ("the reader is a prototype") but never hedges the readings. It presents Maya's placement as self-evidently correct — "That is a specific objection, at a specific address" — when the repo's own verification note says nobody qualified has checked it. That is the honest hedge this chapter is missing, and it is cheap: one clause.

## 2. Is the fictional labelling honest and early enough? (arc decision 3)

No. It fails on both timing and wording.

**Timing.** The disclosure is the second-to-last sentence, at word ~500 of 555. Everything before it is written to be believed: "Nine people contributed," "Take Maya's. She wrote that she is sympathetic…", "She does mention that," "Ana asked where art, celebration, grief ritual… fit." The reader has by then spent four paragraphs forming an impression of two named women's minds. Retracting that in a subordinate clause at the end is the structure of a correction, not a label. Decision 3 says "labelled as fictional contributors, a real AI reading, and a prototype" — that is a label on the exhibit, and labels go on the front.

**Wording.** "Rehearsal, not the room" does not say fictional. On the plainest reading it means a real practice run with real people who were not the intended room — which is exactly the wrong impression, and it is the impression the draft has spent 500 words building. B4's source note gets this right in its own voice: "The contributors in that batch are fictional and the demo says so; the reading is real." B5 has the weaker word in the later position. Say "invented", "made up" or "fictional". The series has already established this move honestly — decision 1 makes B1 say plainly that Maren is composite and invented — so the reader will not be shocked, and doing it *late and softly here* is inconsistent with how the series treats its own inventions everywhere else.

The fix is one sentence, moved and strengthened, at the top: something like *"The contributions below are invented — nine fictional people, written by a colleague, not a transcript of a real session. The reading is real: we gave them to the reader we built and this is what came back."* That sentence also does useful work, because it sets up the honest frame ("what is real here is the reading") that the rest of the chapter needs and currently lacks. Paying it early costs you nothing rhetorically; the run is still interesting.

## 3. Does "pull requests for discourse" sit as a skippable aside? (arc decision 4)

As a sentence, yes — it is one clause, explicitly flagged "For a technical reader," and a non-technical reader loses nothing by skipping it. That much is decision 4 satisfied.

But the *metaphor* has crept out of the aside and into the frame, in three places the aside doesn't govern. "Nothing in that run was **merged**" is the first half of the chapter's bolded key claim. "The **merge** rules are a draft" is in the honesty paragraph. And the aside itself is welded onto the key-claim sentence, in the same paragraph, at the structural climax — the single most prominent position in the piece, which is not where you put a thing you have decided is a skippable aside. Net effect: a reader who does not know git meets "merged" twice as load-bearing vocabulary with no gloss, and a reader who does know git experiences the git framing as the chapter's spine.

Fix: keep the aside, give it its own line or parenthesis away from the bold, and use plain words in the two places outside it. "Nothing in that run was **added to the tree**." "The rules for what gets accepted are a draft." Then "it is pull requests for discourse" is a wink for the people who'll enjoy it, which is what decision 4 asked for.

## 4. Does it show the AI turn? (evaluation finding 5)

Largely yes, and this is the draft's real achievement. Finding 5's complaint was that the load-bearing claim "gets one bold line" and needs "a worked example (a real thread, three proposed placements)". The draft gives one placement worked in full with its alternative and the reason for the ranking, one refusal worked in full with its reason, and an aggregate over the batch. A reader finishes it knowing concretely what the output of this thing looks like. That is a genuine answer to the finding and it should survive revision intact.

Two things still shade toward assertion.

The reader never sees the *shape* of what went in or came out. "A paragraph each" is the only characterisation of the input, and the output is always narrated ("the reading came back as…") rather than shown. The one italicised line is the strongest moment in the chapter precisely because it is the artefact rather than a description of it. Consider one more such moment — the operation and target in their bare form, e.g. that the proposal is literally *challenge the goal, G-1, confidence high* — because the flatness of the actual output is more persuasive than prose about it.

And "a model" is vague where the rest of the chapter is specific. You built a skill, ran a script, and produced a file. B4 says "We have built the reader and run it." B5 retreats to "a model", which sounds like an API call. Naming the thing you built is both more honest and more impressive.

## 5. Is Ana's unplaced contribution earned as "the useful failure"?

This is the weakest argument in the chapter, and the paragraph most likely to be attacked by exactly the reader you want. Five problems, in ascending order of seriousness.

**It is asserted, not demonstrated.** "That is the useful failure" is the draft *telling* the reader how to score the event, one sentence after the event. The aphorism that follows — "A contribution the tree cannot hold is not noise; it is the tree telling you where it is thin" — is a good line and it is doing the actual arguing, which means the label sentence is redundant and should go (see §7).

**Nothing follows from it.** The claim is that the tree "tells you where it is thin". Where does that information go? `review.status` on PROP-009 is `pending`, like all the others. No node is added, no gap register is described, nobody is named as the person who acts on an unplaced item. A finding that produces no consequence is not yet evidence of usefulness; it is a promise. The chapter has the material to close this — the build has a "Record as a gap" affordance and a strip of accepted gaps — and one clause saying what happens to an unplaced contribution would convert the assertion into a mechanism.

**The decline is softer than the draft says.** "So the model declined to place it" reads as a clean refusal. In the YAML, PROP-009 carries `confidence: medium` and an alternative pointing at CSF-1, also `medium` — so the reader was *hedged between* refusing and filing it under durable embodiment, at equal stated confidence. That is a materially different and less flattering fact, and it is one the chapter can afford: a reader who is told the refusal itself was a close call trusts the rest of the paragraph more.

**It credits the reader with Ana's own insight — and this is the most serious of the five.** PROP-009's `interpretation.note` says: "Ana names the three possibilities herself, and the third is the one taken here. Her own uncertainty is part of the contribution rather than a gap in it." Ana's contribution literally ends "I'm not sure whether this should become a condition in the tree, evidence for embodiment, or whether it points to something the model simply doesn't represent yet." The AI picked option three from a menu the contributor supplied. The draft presents this as the machine independently recognising the limits of the structure — "the model declined to place it, and said why" — with no mention that Ana had already put the answer on the table. That is a misattribution of the run's most impressive-looking moment, it is the kind of thing a sceptic finds in ten minutes with the YAML, and finding it would retroactively poison every other claim in the chapter. It is also, I'd argue, *more* interesting told straight: the contributor flagged her own uncertainty and the reader respected it instead of flattening it, which is a better story about the division of labour than a machine having a hunch. (It does also cut against "nobody naming a node or learning a notation" — Ana is reasoning explicitly about the tree's structure. See §1B.)

**It is a staged beat, inherited without acknowledgement.** The YAML says so itself: "The unplaced is last by choice, not by drift: the deck's Demo B ends on the same beat, and a contribution the tree cannot hold is the strongest note to leave a room on." `DECK.md` records the same choice. The batch was ordered for dramatic effect in a talk, and the draft adopts the ordering and the emphasis as though it were the natural shape of the data. I am not asking you to confess the running order. I am asking you to notice that the chapter is currently one-ninth failure presented as the headline, which is a rhetorical choice made for a room and not obviously the right one for a cold reader.

And the adversarial version of the whole paragraph, which the draft never faces: a reader who is counting sees one contribution the system could not place at all and three more it was only medium-confident about — four of nine where a human has to do real work. The draft converts the one clean miss into a feature and never mentions the three mediums as a cost at all (they appear only in the neutral tally sentence). You do not have to concede the point, but you should not leave it unaddressed in the one chapter whose job is evidence. Honest framing is also better framing here: *most of the batch came back with a placement and a confidence; a third came back hedged; one came back with nowhere to go — and that last one is the interesting case.*

## 6. Overclaim audit (evaluation finding 6): every sentence about what has changed

**"and the filing still gets done" — false, and it breaks the handover contract.** Nothing was filed. Nine proposals sit at `review.status: pending`; the draft says so itself eight lines earlier. B4's source note is explicit and underlined: "A build or an edit must not turn 'proposes a placement' into 'files it'." This sentence does exactly that, in the chapter's final sentence, which is the one a reader carries away. It is also self-contradicting within the same page. Must fix.

**The cost that moved is dropped.** B4's source note also requires that "the review load lands on whoever maintains the tree" not be dropped, and gives the reason: "Series A teaches that a constraint moves when you exploit it; a chapter of this series declaring one eliminated would contradict its own companion." B5 says "people decide what goes in" and "the same review step" — both about *who has authority*, neither about *who pays*. And then the closing sentence accounts only for Maren's side of the ledger: "the cost of contributing is back to what it always was… and the filing still gets done," which is precisely the "eliminated" reading B4 forbade. This is the second must-fix, and it is the same sentence. One clause repairs both: *"the cost of contributing is back to what it always was — writing a sentence you mean — and the work that is left is review, which lands on whoever keeps the tree."*

**"That is a specific objection, at a specific address, and anybody can now agree or disagree with it there."** Two overclaims in one sentence. The objection does not yet have an address in the tree — it has a pending proposal in a YAML file; the address is *proposed*, which is the distinction the whole chapter turns on. And "anybody can now" implies the tree is somewhere a stranger can reach and argue in; nothing in the chapter, or the repo, establishes that. Recast as the conditional it actually is: *"If it goes in, it goes in at a specific address, and anyone can then agree or disagree with it there."* You lose nothing — the conditional is still the point.

**"And it runs the other way too. A tree that knows what it holds can tell the next person arriving that this ground has been covered, and point them at the thread where it was."** Present tense, and unsupported by anything in the run. `proposals.yaml` has no back-link to a thread, no provenance field pointing at a source conversation, nothing. The arc asks for "one sentence on the tree pointing the forum back at earlier threads", so the beat belongs here — but it is the one paragraph in an evidence chapter that is pure capability claim, sitting undefended between two paragraphs of demonstrated fact, where it does maximum damage to the reader's trust in the demonstrated parts. Mark it as the plan it is ("the same map should be able to…", "next: …") or cut it. Also "it" has no clear antecedent: the run? the tree? the reader?

**"back to what it always was"** — mild, but "back to" presupposes it had risen, and for Maren it never did; she posted on a forum. The counterfactual is B4's ("had she done it herself, it would have been a morning's work"), so "what it always was" is doing work the reader may not still be holding. Consider "the cost of contributing is what it always was."

**"Nothing in that run was merged"** — accurate, and the most valuable hedge in the draft. Keep.

**"None of this is finished. The trees are provisional and wrong in places, the reader is a prototype, and the merge rules are a draft."** Honest, well-calibrated, and the right length. Keep. Add the missing fourth item (§1: the readings themselves have not been checked by anyone with standing over the tree) — the sentence has room.

## 7. Sentences doing no work, or existing to sound good

- **"Here is the run."** Cut. It is the chapter's opening line — the most valuable sentence in a piece read on a phone — and it is a stage direction. It announces a thing rather than starting it; the next sentence starts it anyway. Worse, a reader arriving from an index rather than from B4 has no idea what run. Replace with the honest frame (§2) and a hinge back to B4's promise.
- **"That is the useful failure."** Cut. Pre-labels the judgement the next sentence earns. Voice-guide item 1: cut the aphorism, keep the claim — here it's cut the label, keep the argument.
- **"in the way people contribute to sessions"** Cut. Near-tautological, and it is the vehicle for the chapter's biggest factual problem (§1A/1B).
- **"And it runs the other way too."** Cut or rewrite. Vague antecedent, and it introduces the unsupported paragraph (§6).
- **"What is true is that…"** Cut the frame. "What is true is that X" advertises a hedge and then delivers the chapter's largest overclaim, which is the worst possible pairing. Say the thing.
- **"Take Maya's."** Keep — three words, does real work, exactly the register the voice guide wants.
- **"She does mention that."** Keep — four words that verify the alternative reading has a basis in the text. Cheap and honest.
- **"A contribution the tree cannot hold is not noise; it is the tree telling you where it is thin."** Keep, as the chapter's one indulgence. It is a not-X-but-Y turn of phrase and the voice guide is suspicious of those, but this one carries a claim that isn't available in plainer words, and it earns the position. One per chapter is the budget; the draft currently spends it twice (this and "That is the useful failure"), so cutting the other one protects this one.

## 8. As the series ending

**Point 4** ("a group's thinking only accumulates if each claim has an address, and AI now makes it affordable to give it one without making contributors do the filing") is landed, and landed by demonstration rather than assertion, which is the whole reason this chapter exists. That is the win. But the final sentence's overclaim (§6) distorts exactly the clause that matters — "without making contributors do the filing" becomes "and the filing gets done," which is a different and untrue claim.

**Points 1–3 are unreachable from here.** They are Series A's, and the arc says A is "the go-deeper path". B5 is the last chapter of the series the arc puts *first on the index* — so for most readers it is the end of the road — and it contains no link to A, no mention that a second series exists, and no hint of what is in it. B4 links to A7; B2 and B3 link to A5. B5, the handoff point, hands off to nothing. A reader who finishes here can say point 4 and has never been told points 1–3 are available. One sentence fixes it, and it is more inviting than the current close: the reader who has just been shown a tree of claims is at their most curious about where trees come from.

**Maren is gone.** The arc names her the running character of the series; B4 ends on her ("nothing is asked of her that was not asked before"); B5 never mentions her. The finale drops its protagonist and substitutes two strangers who — §2 — turn out at the end to be invented. Bringing her back for one clause would cost eight words, close the loop the series opened in B1, and provide the warmth the close currently lacks (voice item 10).

**The close is a command, not an invitation.** "So: find the claim you disagree with. Or find the one nobody is working on." Two imperatives, introduced by a colon that functions as a drumroll. The arc's phrasing is the raw material, not the finished sentence. Three specific failures: (i) it is grammatically an order, and voice item 10 says close on an image that invites "rather than a punchline or a command"; (ii) it has no object — find it *where*? There is no link, no tree, no forum named anywhere in the chapter, so it is an invitation the reader cannot accept even if they want to; (iii) it asks the reader to go and use a thing the preceding paragraph has just described as provisional, prototype and draft, with no account of what would happen if they did. An invitation names the door and holds it open: where the tree is, that it is argued-with rather than finished, what happens to a disagreement once it is written down, and who is on the other end. Warmth here is not decoration — it is the difference between an ask and an instruction, and this is the last thing anyone reads.

## 9. Voice-guide checklist, box by box

- **Could a reader who has never heard of LTP say the one or two things it set out to say?** Partly. They could say "AI reads what people wrote and proposes where it goes; people decide." They would also say two things that are not true: that nine real people were at a session (§1A), and that the filing now gets done (§6). A checklist box that produces one right answer and two wrong ones fails.
- **Is each concept stated plainly before (or as) it is illustrated?** No. The chapter opens on "Here is the run" with no statement of what is being demonstrated or why; the key claim arrives in bold at word ~370, after the evidence. For an evidence chapter, showing before naming is a defensible inversion — but it needs one plain framing sentence at the top, which the draft does not have, and the phone reader arriving cold is the reader the arc names.
- **Any sentence whose job is to sound good rather than to say something?** Yes: "Here is the run.", "That is the useful failure.", "What is true is that…", "And it runs the other way too." Listed with reasons in §7.
- **"We" voice?** Yes, correctly and sparingly ("we gave those paragraphs…"). **Lineage credited?** Not applicable here and rightly absent — this chapter's lineage is the previous chapter. **AI named where it is the enabler?** Yes, but as "a model", which is vaguer than B4's "the reader" and vaguer than the truth (§4).
- **One bolded key claim at most per section?** One bold block, but it is two sentences and the first ("Nothing in that run was merged") is not the key claim — it is the hedge. Bolding the hedge and the claim together lets the reader's eye take the pair as one assertion. Bold the second sentence only.
- **Every hedge honest, no overclaim, the opposing view stated fairly?** No. The honesty paragraph is genuinely good and genuinely calibrated; the sentence immediately after it undoes it (§6), the one-in-nine miss is reframed as a feature rather than costed (§5), the three medium-confidence readings are never named as a cost, and the repo's own verification note — no one with standing has checked whether the readings are right — is not reflected anywhere. The opposing view ("this thing gets it wrong often enough that review is real work") is the obvious objection to this chapter and it is not stated at all.
- **300–600 words?** Yes: 555.

## 10. What is working — do not break these in revision

- **The Maya walkthrough (lines 15–19) is the best thing in the series so far**, and it is the direct answer to evaluation finding 5. The sequence — her words, then the reading, then "Not that the goal is wrong — that it cannot rule anything out", then the alternative and why it lost — is exactly the right amount of machinery shown, in the right order, at the right speed.
- **The italicised interpretation statement** is the moment the chapter stops describing and starts exhibiting. It is also lifted accurately. More of this, not less.
- **"Not that the goal is wrong — that it cannot rule anything out."** A twelve-word rendering of a subtle distinction, in plain language. This is the voice guide's item 1 done right.
- **"and it says so, rather than choosing silently."** The single most persuasive clause in the chapter about why this approach is trustworthy, and it costs six words.
- **"A contribution the tree cannot hold is not noise; it is the tree telling you where it is thin."** Keep (§7).
- **The honesty paragraph ("None of this is finished…")**, minus its missing fourth item. Three concrete admissions, no throat-clearing, no apology. This is item 8 of the voice guide working.
- **The fidelity of the detail.** Almost every specific — the confidence split, the alternative reading, the nearest address, the instrumentalising objection, "nothing merged" — is correct against the source. The problems in §1 are framing problems around an accurate core, which means they are cheap to fix.
