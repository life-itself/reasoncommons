# Critique — B3, *Give every claim an address*

Adversarial read of `draft.md`, 2026-09-20, against `voice-guide.md`, `04-arc-v2.md` (Series B, "The reader", "What they should be able to say afterwards", A5 and A7), `01-evaluation.md` (findings 2, 3 and 6), and the approved `b2-nowhere-to-put-it/script.md`. Prose only; illustrations not judged and none proposed.

Verdict up front: this is the cleanest draft in the series so far on the thing that was most likely to go wrong. Finding 3 — the logic bug where Maren appeared to deny the condition she was actually qualifying — is fixed, twice over. Finding 6's overclaim is fixed, and replaced by a better sentence ("it has an address before anyone has agreed with it"). Finding 2's five-diagram pile-up is gone. But the chapter does not do the one thing its title promises: **it never shows an address.** B2's critique flagged that debt explicitly — "the reader never sees an address, so 'address' stays a word … and B3 is going to build directly on top of it" — and B3 does not pay it. The word "address" appears once in 472 words of body. What the chapter delivers instead is a competent definition of a tree, taught abstractly for four paragraphs before anything concrete arrives, with the load-bearing necessity sentence stated as a formula and never once exercised. And it ends on a trailer for chapter 4 that the published form already prints as a card.

Body word count: **472** (lines 11–27, excluding the header block and the key-claim line). In range, mid-band, about 128 words of headroom.

## 1. The restatement test, and the build-one test

**Key claim:** *A tree of claims gives every claim, and every link between them, a place you can point to — so Maren's suggestion has somewhere to land.*

**Can the reader say it back?** Half of it. They will come off the chapter able to say something like *"you write the goal at the top, break it into the things that have to be true for it, and then any particular point has a spot on the diagram."* That is the first clause and the last clause. The middle clause — *and every link between them* — will not survive. The draft asserts it ("A link is a claim too, and it can be wrong on its own") and then never demonstrates it: no link in the chapter is pointed at, named, disputed, or given a place. Maren's contribution would have been the perfect demonstration — a proposed *link* between an existing condition and a new one is exactly a claim about necessity — but the draft describes what she adds as "a proposed new condition", a box, not the arrow. The half of the key claim that is genuinely non-obvious, and that distinguishes a tree from an outline, is the half that goes undemonstrated.

**Could they build a small tree of their own?** They could *attempt* one. They could not *check* one. The chapter gives them a procedure (write the goal; ask what has to be true; repeat) and withholds every judgement the procedure needs:

- **How do I tell necessary from merely helpful?** Stated once, as a definition — "not everything that would help, but the conditions without which it fails" — and never applied. There is no instance anywhere in the chapter of a candidate being *rejected* for being helpful-but-not-necessary. A definition without a worked rejection is a definition a reader agrees with and cannot use. A4 has the instance this chapter needs and lacks (fire needs fuel, heat *and* oxygen — a match is helpful, not necessary).
- **When do I stop?** "Keep going as far down as is useful." That is the writer declining to answer. A reader building their first tree will hit this within ninety seconds.
- **What does a claim look like?** The 2R conditions are decent models ("people come to understand the crises as one crisis" — a sentence, not a topic), but nothing tells the reader that a node is a proposition and not a subject heading. The single commonest failure when a newcomer builds their first tree is writing nouns in the boxes, and one clause would head it off.

So: partial pass on restatement, fail on construction. Given that the arc's promise for Series B is item 4 — *a group's thinking only accumulates if each claim has an address* — that may be acceptable (B3 does not have to be a tutorial, and A5 is the tutorial). But then the chapter should not spend its first three paragraphs in tutorial register, because that is a promise it does not keep.

## 2. The necessity idea — the load-bearing sentence, attacked hardest

> The links matter as much as the boxes: each one says *in order to have this, we need that*.

This is the sentence the whole method rests on, and it is the most skippable sentence on the page. Four problems, in order of severity.

**It is a formula with two pronouns and no referents.** "This" and "that" are placeholders. The reader has, at this point in the chapter, no instance in their head — the Second Renaissance does not arrive for two more paragraphs — so there is nothing for the pronouns to bind to. The italics signal *this is the important bit* while the content is a shape with the values missing. Voice guide direction 3 asks for the concept stated plainly and *then shown*. Here it is stated plainly and then not shown until much later, by which time the formula is gone.

**It is buried in a four-idea paragraph.** Paragraph 3 introduces, in three sentences: what a tree is, what a root is, why claims hang where they hang, that links matter as much as boxes, the necessity formula, that a link is itself a claim, and that a link can be false while the claim below it is true. That is seven moves. The one that matters most is fourth in the queue, mid-paragraph, on the hardest screen of the chapter for a phone reader.

**The necessity idea is actually explained better elsewhere in the draft, and the draft doesn't notice.** Two sentences do the job the formula fails at:

> Then ask what has to be true for it to happen — not everything that would help, but the conditions without which it fails.

> Underneath it sit eight conditions, all of which have to hold at once.

"All of which have to hold at once" is the plainest statement of necessity in the chapter, it is attached to a real example, and it arrives in paragraph 5 with no signal that it is the same idea as the italicised formula in paragraph 3. The reader meets necessity three times in three different vocabularies — *conditions without which it fails*, *in order to have this, we need that*, *all of which have to hold at once* — and is never told these are one idea. Pick one phrasing and use it throughout, including for Maren's card (see §3).

**"That is the whole grammar" then closes the subject before it was opened.** The reader has had one abstract formula; the chapter tells them they now have the complete system. A reader who did not follow the formula is now told there is nothing more to follow, which is the worst possible instruction — it converts confusion into the reader's fault rather than the page's. See §5.

**Does the explanation only feel plain to someone who already knows?** Paragraphs 2 and 3 in isolation: yes, mostly plain — short words, short sentences, no jargon beyond "root". The failure is not vocabulary, it is that every sentence is a *rule* and none is an *instance*. Someone who already holds a goal tree in their head reads those two paragraphs and thinks "yes, exactly, and admirably compressed". Someone who does not reads five imperatives and an italicised formula and has nothing to test them against. That is the standard insider illusion, and it survives here precisely because the prose is clean.

One further trap: **"The claim at the top is the root."** A tree with its root at the top is counter-intuitive to anyone who has seen a tree. The draft states it and moves on. "Root" is then never used again in the chapter — nothing later depends on it — so it is a confusing word that buys nothing. Either cut it or spend four words acknowledging that this one grows downward.

## 3. Maren — does her point land where it should?

**On the specific bug, this is a pass, and worth saying clearly.** Finding 3 was that the earlier piece crossed out the "small dense groups" condition's link to the goal, which reads as Maren denying that condition. The draft never does this. It attaches her point *under* the condition, as a proposed extra condition that qualifies it, and it says so twice. It also gets the shape right: a proposal, not an amendment; not yet agreed; hanging under the node it qualifies. The arc asked for exactly this and the draft delivers it.

Four problems remain, none of them the old bug.

**The negation is doing the work, and negation is a weak instrument.** Two sentences tell the reader what Maren is *not* doing — "She was not objecting to the strategy in general" and "She does not have to reject the whole strategy in order to say something" — before either tells them what she *is* doing. This is the writer defending against a charge the reader never brought, and it plants the rejected reading in the reader's head twice. Lead with the positive: *her sentence is a proposal that one of the eight conditions needs a further condition underneath it.* Then one negation, if any, is enough.

**Her sentence is not requoted, and it should be.** It is 22 words. The reader last saw it in chapter 1, possibly on another day, possibly not at all if they arrived on a link to B3. "Now look again at Maren's sentence" invites a reader to look at something that is not on the page. The chapter's whole payoff is that *this specific sentence* lands in *that specific place*, and one of the two terms is missing.

**The draft asserts an interpretation as though it were a reading-off.** Maren wrote: *"The theory of change seems to float free of any actual place. You should start in a neighbourhood somebody actually lives in."* The draft renders this as *"the second of those conditions is incomplete: small dense groups make a new way of living livable only if they are rooted in a place somebody actually lives in."* That is a defensible reading. It is not the only one — her sentence reads at least as naturally as an objection to the *top* claim or to the theory of change as a whole, which is nearer what she literally wrote. The chapter presents the placement as obvious, in the flat indicative ("She was saying that…"), and in doing so hides the interpretive step that B5 exists to be honest about (AI proposes a placement, *with a confidence and an alternative reading*; people decide). One clause would fix it and strengthen the chapter: something to the effect that this is one reading of what she meant, and the gain is that the reading is now a thing somebody can argue with. As printed, B3 quietly claims the hard part is easy, and B5 will then have to un-claim it.

**"Only if" is not the grammar the chapter just taught.** Three paragraphs earlier the reader was given *in order to have this, we need that*. Maren's card — the chapter's single worked instance of that grammar — is phrased in the converse form, "X only if Y", leaving the reader to perform the equivalence unaided. Use the taught words: *in order for small dense groups to make a new way of living livable, we need them rooted in a place somebody actually lives in.* Then the formula from paragraph 3 has finally been exercised, at the payoff, on the reader's behalf.

Minor, same paragraph: **"the second of those conditions"** makes the reader count backwards through a list of two-plus-six. Name it.

## 4. Overclaim about what happens next

The chapter mostly holds the line, and one sentence is a genuine improvement on the published version. The published piece said "anyone can see exactly what it would change"; the draft says:

> and it has an address before anyone has agreed with it.

That is the honest version, and it is the best sentence in the chapter. Keep it and, per §7, consider bolding it.

**Then the next paragraph breaks the rule.**

> Something has changed for her as well as for the movement.

Nothing has changed for the movement. At this point in the series the tree exists and Maren's card hangs off it as a proposal; nothing has been discussed, agreed or merged. B5 is where anything is decided, and even there the arc's line is "people decide what goes in", not "it went in". This is finding 6 in a quieter costume — the old draft said "she has changed what the movement believes" for a merged sentence, and this one says something has changed for the movement on the strength of an unagreed proposal. It is also, structurally, a topic sentence announcing what the paragraph is about to demonstrate, which the paragraph then demonstrates only for *her*. Cut the "as well as for the movement" half, or replace it with what is actually true: the movement now has a place where a disagreement about this would show up.

> And when someone replies, they are replying to *that*, at a place both of them can return to in a year and find again.

Softer, but it assumes three things not yet in evidence: that someone replies, that the tree is being maintained a year later, and that the address is stable under revision. "Can return to in a year and find again" is precisely the statute property from B2 — which the chapter has not demonstrated for trees (see §6). As a conditional it is fine; as a flat future indicative it is a promise the series has not yet earned. The fix is one word of mood, not a rewrite.

Otherwise clean. "None of this is our invention" and "used on factories and hospitals for decades" are honest and calibrated. AI is correctly absent — B3 does not reach forward for B4's turn, and that restraint is right.

## 5. Sentences doing no work, or existing to sound good

> That is the whole grammar. It takes about a minute to explain, which is the point: the reader of the tree does not have to be trained to argue with it.

The prime suspect, and it deserves the suspicion. Three moves, two of which are about the writing rather than the world. **"That is the whole grammar"** grades the completeness of the exposition. **"It takes about a minute to explain, which is the point"** grades its brevity — and is a claim about how long the chapter took, not about trees. "Grammar" is a metaphor introduced here and dropped immediately. The only content is the final clause, and that content is real and valuable: *you do not have to be trained to argue with a tree.* Say that, plainly, as its own sentence, and delete the two gradings in front of it. Also note the sentence is doing damage as well as no work: telling a reader who has just met an abstract formula that they now have the whole system forecloses the instance they needed.

> There is a way to give reasoning that property, and it is older than any of the software.

Two faults. **"That property"** asks the reader to carry forward, across a chapter break, a property B2 described in four clauses about statutes — and does not restate it, so the sentence opens on a pronoun with no antecedent on the page. **"older than any of the software"** is a flourish: which software? The chapter has mentioned none. It also sits oddly against the honest lineage line at the end ("for decades"), which is the same fact said concretely. Cut the clause; name the property.

> The tree is where a claim lives.

Cadence. The chapter has just spent three paragraphs demonstrating this; restating it in six flat words is a curtain line, not information. Its only function is to set up the swerve that follows, which should go too (§6).

> drawing it out of the movement's own written corpus rather than starting fresh

"Corpus" is a register break — B2 said "the papers", "six or seven of them", "close to a hundred thousand words", in that plain vocabulary. "Rather than starting fresh" is doing real and honest work and should stay.

> and we say more about how these trees are built — and how you check a link without taking anyone's word for it — in the companion series

Not flourish, but admin, and it is the last substantive thing the reader is told before the close. It is also vague where the arc is specific: the goal tree is **A5** (the arc names B3 as the chapter that links to it) and checking a link is **A4**. A reader told "in the companion series" has to go hunting. Point at the chapter.

> Keep going as far down as is useful.

Not a flourish, but it is a sentence that appears to give guidance and gives none. Either give a stopping rule (stop when the next condition down is something you could actually act on, or something nobody disputes) or admit there isn't a clean one.

## 6. Does it pay off B2's ending?

**No — and this is the chapter's largest structural gap.** B2 ends on a precise, three-part property, deliberately set up as the thing the next chapter must supply:

> A section of a statute has a citation. Everything that relies on it cites that citation, and when the section is amended, everything pointing at it now points at the new text. That is exactly what an address gives you. Reasoning does not have one.

Three properties: **(a)** the thing has a citation — an identity you can utter; **(b)** other things point at that identity; **(c)** when it changes, what points at it points at the new version. Score the draft:

- **(a) Identity: absent.** Nothing in the chapter is ever pointed at. No claim gets a name, a number, a handle, or a quotable form. The reader who finished B2 asking "so what does an address actually *look* like?" finishes B3 still asking. This is the second consecutive chapter to leave the title concept unshown, and B2's critique warned that B3 would be building on top of the gap.
- **(b) Others point at it: partial.** The tree's links are pointing, and the draft says links are claims. But no *instance* of one claim pointing at another is exhibited beyond the tree's own structure, and Maren's card — the obvious instance — is described as a box rather than as an arrow (§1).
- **(c) Amendment propagates: absent.** Nothing in the chapter says what happens to the tree when a claim changes. This is the property that makes an address an address rather than a filing slot, it is the one B2 spent its ending on, and B3 does not mention it. It is also cheap to supply: if the "small dense groups" condition is rewritten, everything hanging under it — including Maren's proposal — is still hanging under it, and everyone arguing about it is still arguing about the same node.

And the word itself: **"address" appears exactly once in the body** ("it has an address before anyone has agreed with it"), in a subordinate clause, in paragraph 6, in a chapter titled *Give every claim an address*. The draft's own substitute vocabulary — "a place you can point to", "where a claim lives", "somewhere to land" — is softer and less specific than the word B2 spent a whole chapter earning. Thread it back through.

## 7. The last line — ending, or trailer?

> The tree is where a claim lives. What it does not yet explain is why, if it is this simple, hardly anybody does it.

**A trailer, and a redundant one.** The second sentence is B4's key claim rephrased as a tease, and the published form already prints B4's title and teaser as a next-chapter card directly beneath the prose (see `form-prototype/03-give-every-claim-an-address/index.md`). The reader gets the same handoff twice, once coyly and once properly. B2's critique praised that draft specifically for refusing to trail B3 — "no 'next chapter', no naming of trees, no promise" — and called it a decision a revision must not undo. This draft undoes it.

It also has a small self-inflicted wound: two paragraphs after saying "*we* built one", the chapter asks why "hardly anybody does it". The reader's honest reaction is *you just said you did*. B4 resolves that (it was expensive), but the last line of B3 invites the objection with nothing to answer it.

Two endings are already sitting in the draft, either of which would close the chapter properly. **"It has an address before anyone has agreed with it"** is the chapter's best sentence and closes the loop straight back to B2's statute. Or the Maren paragraph's real payoff — *she has to argue that one condition is missing, which is a far smaller and far more answerable thing to argue* — which is the chapter's actual gift to the reader and ends on a person rather than an abstraction. The lineage paragraph, if kept, should go before whichever ending is chosen, not after.

## 8. The voice-guide checklist, box by box

- [~] **Could a reader who has never heard of LTP say, after this chapter, the one or two things it set out to say?** Partially. They can say a tree gives each claim a spot and that Maren's point now has one. They cannot say what an address *is*, cannot say that links are addressable too, and cannot build or check a tree. §1, §6.
- [ ] **Is each concept stated plainly before (or as) it is illustrated?** Stated plainly, yes; illustrated, no. The tree is defined for three paragraphs before any instance arrives, and the necessity link — the load-bearing concept — is never illustrated at all, including at the one place it obviously could be (Maren's card, which switches to "only if"). The first concrete noun in the chapter is "the Second Renaissance", roughly 180 words in. For a phone reader arriving cold this is the most abandonable chapter in the series so far. §2.
- [ ] **Any sentence whose job is to sound good rather than to say something?** At least four, quoted in §5: "That is the whole grammar. It takes about a minute to explain, which is the point"; "older than any of the software"; "The tree is where a claim lives"; and the trailer that follows it.
- [x] **"We" voice; lineage credited where a method is used; AI named where it is the enabler.** Pass, and comfortably. Three "we" forms, all honest. The Logical Thinking Process is named where the method is borrowed, with an honest provenance ("factories and hospitals for decades") and an honest disclaimer ("None of this is our invention"). AI correctly absent — that is B4 and B5, and the draft does not reach. The only repair needed is pointing the companion-series link at A5 rather than at "the companion series" in general.
- [~] **One bolded key claim at most per section.** Zero in the body. Numerically compliant with "at most", but it breaks the form B1 and B2 both use, and it means the chapter has no sentence a reader would screenshot or quote back. The candidate is already written: *it has an address before anyone has agreed with it* — or a fuller version of it.
- [ ] **Every hedge honest, no overclaim, the opposing view stated fairly.** Fails on two of three. Overclaim: "Something has changed for her as well as for the movement" (§4), and "they are replying to *that*, at a place both of them can return to in a year" stated as fact. Opposing view: **absent entirely.** The informed reader has two loud objections and the chapter acknowledges neither — *people have tried argument mapping, issue trees, mind maps and debate platforms, and they mostly die*, and *who decides where a claim belongs, and what if the placement is wrong?* The second is especially costly because the draft's own handling of Maren's sentence is the live example of a contestable placement (§3). B2's critique made the identical finding about tooling and the fix was twelve words. Facts themselves check out: eight conditions and the corpus-derived tree match `second-renaissance-plain/index.md`.
- [x] **300–600 words.** 472 in the body. Mid-band, ~128 words spare, and §10 says where to spend them.

## 9. What is working — protect this in revision

- **The Maren paragraph's logic.** "She was saying that the second of those conditions is incomplete … That is a proposed new condition, hanging under the one it qualifies." The bug from finding 3 is properly dead: the condition is qualified, not denied; the proposal hangs *under* the thing it qualifies; nothing is crossed out. This is the thing the chapter most had to get right and it got it right. Any rewrite must preserve the geometry exactly.
- **"and it has an address before anyone has agreed with it."** The best sentence on the page. It fixes finding 6 (nothing has been merged), it is the honest version of the published piece's "anyone can see exactly what it would change", and it is the only place the title's word does real work. It is also a candidate ending and a candidate bold.
- **"she has to argue that one condition is missing, which is a far smaller and far more answerable thing to argue."** The chapter's real payoff, stated flatly, with the benefit to a person rather than to a system. This is the target register.
- **"not everything that would help, but the conditions without which it fails."** The clearest statement of necessity in the draft — it just needs an instance beside it.
- **"Underneath it sit eight conditions, all of which have to hold at once."** Concrete, checkable against the real tree, and the one place necessity is shown rather than defined.
- **"A link is a claim too, and it can be wrong on its own — the thing below might be true and still not be necessary for the thing above."** Genuinely non-obvious, and the thing that makes a tree different from an outline. Undemonstrated (§1), but do not cut it in a compression pass — build on it instead.
- **Finding 2 is not reintroduced.** One tree, no five-diagram sheet, no lineage paragraph in the middle of the argument. The published piece's mistake is cleanly avoided.
- **"drawing it out of the movement's own written corpus rather than starting fresh."** Honest about provenance in a way that quietly sets up B4.
- **Restraint about AI.** B3 could easily have leaked B4's turn. It does not.
- **Length discipline.** 472 words with headroom.

## 10. Ranked fixes

1. **Show one address.** The chapter is titled *Give every claim an address*, B2 ended by defining an address in three exact properties, and B3 supplies none of them. Take one node of the 2R tree — "small dense groups make the new way of living livable" is the obvious one — and give it a visible identity the reader could utter, show one other thing pointing at it (Maren's proposal is right there), and say what happens when the claim is amended: everything pointing at it still points at it. That is B2's statute, delivered. Spend most of the ~128 spare words here. Without this fix the chapter changes the subject from B2 rather than answering it.
2. **Exercise the necessity formula instead of declaring it complete.** Immediately after *in order to have this, we need that*, put one instance with both slots filled, and — critically — one candidate **rejected** for being helpful but not necessary. The definition "the conditions without which it fails" is currently untestable because nothing is ever tested against it. Then use the same words again for Maren's card (fix 4). One phrasing throughout: pick *in order to have this, we need that* and drop the competing vocabularies.
3. **Cut the last two sentences and end on the chapter's own payoff.** "The tree is where a claim lives. What it does not yet explain is why, if it is this simple, hardly anybody does it." — the first is cadence, the second is B4's key claim printed twice, because the form already renders a next-chapter card with B4's title and teaser. End on "it has an address before anyone has agreed with it", or on "a far smaller and far more answerable thing to argue". B2 earned praise for refusing to trail B3; do not lose that here.
4. **Rebuild the Maren paragraph positive-first, with her sentence requoted.** Requote the 22 words (a reader may be arriving cold on a link). Lead with what she is doing — proposing that one of the eight conditions needs a further condition under it — and keep at most one of the two "she is not rejecting the strategy" sentences. Phrase her card in the taught grammar: *in order for small dense groups to make a new way of living livable, we need them rooted in a place somebody actually lives in.* Name the condition instead of "the second of those conditions".
5. **Fix the overclaim: "Something has changed for her as well as for the movement."** Nothing has changed for the movement — nothing is agreed or merged until B5, and even there people decide. Cut the second half, or replace with what is true: the movement now has a place where a disagreement about this would show up. In the same paragraph, put "they are replying to *that*, at a place both of them can return to in a year" into the conditional; as a flat future it promises maintenance the series has not shown.
6. **Cut "That is the whole grammar. It takes about a minute to explain, which is the point" and keep the clause underneath it.** The surviving content — *you do not have to be trained to argue with a tree* — is a real claim and should stand as its own plain sentence. The two sentences in front of it grade the writing, not the world, and they tell a reader who did not follow the formula that there is nothing left to follow.
7. **Bold one sentence in the body.** The chapter currently has nothing a reader would quote. *It has an address before anyone has agreed with it* is the candidate, possibly expanded to carry the whole claim: a proposal gets a place before anyone agrees with it, and that is what makes disagreement cheap. One bold, and it must be the sentence the chapter is for.
8. **Add the missing concession, in one clause.** Either that people have built argument maps, debate platforms and wikis before and most of them died — B4 then explains why, so this is a setup, not a liability — or, better, that where Maren's sentence belongs is a judgement somebody has to make and could get wrong, which is honest, is the live case in this very chapter, and hands B5 its subject. The chapter currently has no opposing view of any kind.
9. **Break up paragraph 3 and demonstrate the link half of the key claim.** Seven moves in three sentences, on the hardest screen of the chapter. Split it, and give "a link is a claim too, and it can be wrong on its own" a visible consequence — the obvious one being that Maren is adding an arrow as much as a box, which is also fix 1's second property and fix 4's payoff.
10. **Repair the opening.** "That property" opens on a pronoun whose antecedent is in another chapter: restate the property in four words. Cut "older than any of the software" — it is a flourish, it names no software, and the honest version of the same fact is already at the foot of the chapter. Consider getting something concrete in front of the reader sooner: 180 words of pure abstraction before the first real noun is the chapter's biggest phone-reading risk.
11. **Small repairs.** "Root" is used once, never again, and makes a tree grow downward without acknowledgement — cut it or spend four words on it. "Corpus" → the plain vocabulary B2 used ("the papers"). "Keep going as far down as is useful" — give a stopping rule or admit there isn't one. Point the companion-series link at **A5** (goal tree) and **A4** (checking a link), which is what the arc specifies, not at "the companion series".
12. **Add a source note for the build.** B1 and B2 both have one; B3 has none, and it makes checkable claims about a real organisation (a tree built earlier this year from the real written corpus, eight conditions, two of them named) plus an interpretive claim about a composite character's meaning. The note should say: the tree and the eight conditions are real; Maren and her sentence are composite; **the placement of her point under "small dense groups" is our reading of her sentence, not a reading-off**, and a build or an edit must not turn it into a claim that the movement has accepted it.
