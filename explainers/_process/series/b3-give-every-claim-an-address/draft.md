# Draft — B3, *Give every claim an address*

Series B, chapter 3 of 5. Bead `reasoncommons-bnl.18`. Prose only, no visual direction. Follows [B2](../b2-nowhere-to-put-it/script.md), which ends on the statutory citation: reasoning has no equivalent.

---

**Key claim:** A tree of claims gives every claim, and every link between them, a place you can point to — so Maren's suggestion has somewhere to land.

---

There is a way to give reasoning that property, and it is older than any of the software.

Take the thing you are trying to achieve and write it down as a single claim. Then ask what has to be true for it to happen — not everything that would help, but the conditions without which it fails. Write each of those down. Then ask the same question of each of them. Keep going as far down as is useful.

What you get is a tree. The claim at the top is the root, and every claim below it is there because something above it depends on it. The links matter as much as the boxes: each one says *in order to have this, we need that*. A link is a claim too, and it can be wrong on its own — the thing below might be true and still not be necessary for the thing above.

That is the whole grammar. It takes about a minute to explain, which is the point: the reader of the tree does not have to be trained to argue with it.

Earlier this year we built one for the Second Renaissance, drawing it out of the movement's own written corpus rather than starting fresh. The root is that a Second Renaissance becomes more than an idea. Underneath it sit eight conditions, all of which have to hold at once: that people come to understand the crises as one crisis, that small dense groups make the new way of living livable, and six more.

Now look again at Maren's sentence. She was not objecting to the strategy in general. She was saying that the second of those conditions is incomplete: small dense groups make a new way of living livable *only if* they are rooted in a place somebody actually lives in. That is a proposed new condition, hanging under the one it qualifies, and it has an address before anyone has agreed with it.

Something has changed for her as well as for the movement. She does not have to reject the whole strategy in order to say something; she has to argue that one condition is missing, which is a far smaller and far more answerable thing to argue. And when someone replies, they are replying to *that*, at a place both of them can return to in a year and find again.

None of this is our invention. It comes from the Logical Thinking Process, which has been used on factories and hospitals for decades, and we say more about how these trees are built — and how you check a link without taking anyone's word for it — in the companion series.

The tree is where a claim lives. What it does not yet explain is why, if it is this simple, hardly anybody does it.
