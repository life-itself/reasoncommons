# Script — A1, *Sixty people waiting*

Series A, chapter 1 of 8. Bead `reasoncommons-bnl.24`. Revised against [`critique.md`](critique.md); the draft is in [`draft.md`](draft.md). Prose only, no visual direction. 463 words.

---

**Key claim:** How much any system achieves is set by one constraint, and it is almost never where people are busiest.

---

The Ashfield clinic opens at eight. By noon, sixty people are waiting.

Everyone who works there can tell you where the problem is, and they all point at the same desk. Maria runs the front desk. The phone starts before the lights are on, there is a queue at her window from opening until close, and when patients write in to complain — they do — they complain about her desk. She is, visibly and unarguably, the hardest-working person in the building. Put the clinic's one spare hire there and check-in gets faster, the queue at the window shortens, and the morning stops feeling like a scrum.

The clinic would still treat twenty patients a day. It has treated twenty patients a day for four years.

Everyone who comes through passes five stations in order, and each has a limit. Check-in can process sixty people a day. Triage, forty-five. Dr Adeyemi, who has been here nine years, sees twenty. Pharmacy, fifty. Follow-up booking, forty.

Twenty is the smallest number, so twenty is the clinic's number. Not an average, not a target — arithmetic. Today the other four capacities are doing no work at all, which does not mean they never will.

Sixty people come through the door. Twenty are seen. The other forty go home. One of them is a man in his sixties, back a second time about a cough he has had since March; he waited three hours on Tuesday, left at four to collect his grandson, and will try again next week. Multiply him — forty a day, five days a week — and something like ten thousand people a year come to a clinic where everybody is working flat out, and do not get seen.

So what is the clinic actually for? Patients seen, and seen well. Not visits logged, not hours worked. The number that matters is how many patients come out the far end.

**The station that sets that number is the constraint. Every system has one, and almost no system knows where its own is.** The idea is Eliyahu Goldratt's, and it is the whole of his Theory of Constraints.

It also explains the busyness. A station that is not the constraint, running flat out, manufactures work the constraint cannot absorb — sixty people checked in against twenty who can be seen. The surplus does not vanish; it sits in the waiting room. When everyone in a place is at full stretch, the honest reading is not that it is well run. It is that nobody has found the constraint, so everybody is compensating for it at once.

Maria was never the problem. She was busy absorbing the anger that something else was generating.

*The Ashfield clinic is a composite. Its numbers are invented, and chosen so you can check the arithmetic.*

## Source note for the build

Every figure is carried over from the published long piece, `explainers/01-bottleneck-plain/index.md`: capacities 60 / 45 / 20 / 50 / 40, Maria on the front desk, Dr Adeyemi nine years in post, twenty a day for four years, the man with the cough in every particular, and 40 × 5 × 50 ≈ 10,000 a year. Keep them identical across A1–A8; the arithmetic is the one thing in this series a reader can check for themselves, and A2's gain to twenty-six depends on it.

**Two things the draft got wrong; do not reintroduce either.** It said "four of those numbers do not matter" — false, since follow-up booking at forty becomes the constraint as soon as the doctor is relieved, which is A8's ground and would have had A2 contradicting A1. And it derived "the other forty go home" from check-in *capacity* without ever saying that sixty people arrive; the script now states demand.

Dr Adeyemi is named here deliberately. A2 asks why she loses two hours a day to admin, A6 answers it, and A8 resolves it with Maria — the question cannot be asked about a stranger.
