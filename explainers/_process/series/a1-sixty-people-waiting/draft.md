# Draft — A1, *Sixty people waiting*

Facts carried over from the published piece (`explainers/01-bottleneck-plain/index.md`), which this compresses: station capacities 60 / 45 / 20 / 50 / 40, Maria on the front desk, Dr Adeyemi nine years in post, twenty patients a day for four years.

---

**Key claim:** How much any system achieves is set by one constraint, and it is almost never where people are busiest.

---

A clinic opens at eight. By noon, sixty people are waiting.

Everyone who works there can tell you exactly where the problem is, and they all point at the same desk. They are all wrong.

Maria runs the front desk. The phone starts before the lights are on, there is a queue at her window from opening until close, and when patients write in to complain — they do — they complain about her desk. She is, visibly and unarguably, the hardest-working person in the building.

She is also irrelevant to how many people this clinic helps.

The clinic treats twenty patients a day. It has treated twenty patients a day for four years.

Here is why. Everybody who comes through passes five stations in order, and each has a limit: check-in can process sixty people a day, triage forty-five, the doctor twenty, pharmacy fifty, follow-up booking forty. Four of those numbers do not matter. The doctor sees twenty, so the clinic sees twenty. It is not an average or a target; it is arithmetic.

The other forty go home. One of them is a man in his sixties, back for the second time about a cough he has had since March. He waited three hours on Tuesday, left at four to collect his grandson, and will try again next week. Multiply him: forty a day, five days a week, something like ten thousand people a year who came to a clinic where everyone was working flat out and did not get seen.

Say what the clinic is for, plainly, because everything else depends on it: patients seen, and seen well. Not visits logged, not hours worked. If that is the goal, then the number that matters is how many patients come out the far end, and exactly one station governs that number.

**The station that sets the number — the one every other station is waiting on, whether it knows it or not — is the constraint. Every system has one. Almost none knows where its own is.**

Maria was never the problem. She was busy absorbing the anger that something else was generating, which is what being next to a constraint usually looks like from the outside.

This is not our idea. It is the heart of Eliyahu Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, and the seven chapters after this one are what you can do with it once you believe it.
