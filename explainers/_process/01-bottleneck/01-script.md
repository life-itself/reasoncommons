# Everybody's Busy — script v1

**Stage 1 of 3.** Pure narrative. No visual direction, no figure slots, no block
numbering. This file exists to be judged as *writing* and nothing else: does the argument
land, does it move, would a stranger finish it.

Read it aloud. If a sentence is hard to say, it's wrong.

Target: ~1,100 words.

---

A clinic opens at eight. By noon, sixty people are waiting.

Everyone who works there can tell you exactly where the problem is. They are all pointing
at the same desk.

They are all wrong.

---

The receptionist works through lunch most days. The phone starts before the lights are on.
Every complaint letter names her desk. She is, visibly and unarguably, the hardest-working
person in the building.

She is also irrelevant to how many people this clinic helps. It treats twenty patients a
day. It has treated twenty patients a day for four years — a number set entirely by
something almost nobody in the building is looking at.

This is not really a story about a clinic.

---

Everyone who comes through passes five stations, in order, and every one has a limit.
Check-in can process sixty people a day. Triage, forty-five. The doctor, twenty. Pharmacy,
fifty. Follow-up booking, forty.

Four of the five stations can handle more than twice what the doctor can.

So the clinic treats twenty people a day. Not eighty-five, which is what its five stations
could manage between them. Twenty.

---

The clinic has just been given enough money to hire one more person.

Put them on the front desk, where the queue is: still twenty. Put them on triage: still
twenty. Pharmacy: twenty. Follow-up: twenty.

There is exactly one place that changes the number, and it is not where the queue is.

Hire at the front desk and check-in gets faster — so patients reach the waiting room
sooner. You will have spent the whole budget to make people frustrated sitting down instead
of standing up.

The receptionist was never the problem. She was busy absorbing the anger that something
else was generating. Busy is not the same as stuck.

---

Now the question that decides whether you have really understood it.

Every single person in an organisation is working flat out, all day. Is that organisation
running at its best?

Almost everyone says yes. It feels less like a claim than like arithmetic.

But think about what full effort at the front desk actually produces. Sixty people checked
in, against twenty the doctor can see. The other forty do not vanish. They sit in the
waiting room, and the waiting room is not output — it is forty people's afternoons, spent.

A station that isn't the limit, running at maximum, manufactures work that the limit cannot
absorb. In a factory that work is inventory, stacked and going stale. On a software team it
is a queue of finished tickets nobody has capacity to review. In the clinic it is people.

So a well-run system needs most of its parts to have slack — deliberately running below
their maximum — so that the one part that governs everything can run flat out.

Everyone working hard is not evidence that things are going well. It is evidence that
nobody has found the constraint.

---

Which produces a fact that is almost impossible to act on.

Picture an organisation doing everything right by conventional lights: a dozen energetic
improvement teams, each working hard on something everybody agrees needs fixing.

At most one of them is working on the thing that limits output. The other eleven are
producing nothing but effort.

---

Back to the clinic. We know it is the doctor. So hire another doctor.

Not yet. Watch her day first.

About two hours of it goes on things that do not need a medical degree — re-taking a
history the triage nurse already took, hunting for notes, printing forms. Move those two
hours off her desk and the clinic goes from twenty patients a day to twenty-eight. Cost:
nothing.

That is the step almost everyone skips: get everything you can out of the limiting part
*before* you spend a penny on it.

The next step is harder, because it looks like failure. Everyone else now runs at whatever
pace keeps the doctor working. Reception deliberately slows down. Every local number you
have will show those people getting worse while the clinic gets better. If you cannot
tolerate that on a dashboard, none of this is available to you.

Only then do you spend money — and you spend it on the doctor, not on the loudest queue.

---

Ten thousand people visit a website this month. Forty sign up. Three people work on the
funnel: one on getting visitors, one on converting them, one on keeping them. Sensibly,
they each try to improve their part.

But if you are improving all three equally, you already know you are being wasteful. Until
conversion moves, another ten thousand visitors is worth almost nothing — and the person
working hardest on traffic is producing the most waste.

A team where every calendar is full and nothing ships, because everything is queued behind
the one person who reviews.

A parent getting older. A dozen worries, all of them real. The thing actually holding
everything up is one phone call nobody has made. Not the biggest problem on the list. The
stuck one.

The question is never *how hard are we pushing?* It is: are we pushing on the thing that's
stuck?

---

Fix the doctor and the clinic does not become fixed.

Get her to fifty a day and the pharmacy, at fifty, becomes the limit. Fix that and it moves
to follow-up booking. Push far enough and every station inside the building outruns the
number of people it serves — at which point the limit has moved outside the walls, to the
hospital that won't take referrals any faster.

It is still a limit. It just now needs an entirely different set of skills to attack.

Which is why this work never finishes, and why the most dangerous sentence anyone in that
clinic can say is *we solved that one*.

---

You may have noticed that all of this was easy. Five boxes, five numbers, find the
smallest.

Real organisations are not like that. The limiting thing is usually not a person or a
machine. It is a rule.

*Every prescription needs the doctor's countersignature.* *No appointment longer than ten
minutes.* *Nothing ships without sign-off.* *Orders go in monthly, to get the discount.*

Every one of those was introduced for a good reason, usually to solve a problem nobody now
remembers. John Gardner described an established organisation as a coral reef of procedures
laid down to achieve some long-forgotten objective.

Breaking one is nearly free — a rule costs nothing to change, next to hiring a doctor — and
it usually improves things more than any purchase would.

And they are invisible, because everybody experiences them as normal. A bottleneck machine
announces itself: there is a pile of work in front of it. A bottleneck rule has no pile. It
hides inside *that's just how it's done*, and it is defended, sincerely, by good people
whose own numbers depend on it.

---

So: find the constraint. Put nearly everything into it. Ignore, for now, almost everything
else.

But if the constraint is usually a rule — invisible, normal-seeming, defended in good
faith, connected to the symptoms you *can* see by long chains of cause and effect that
cross every boundary on the org chart — then how would you ever find one?

Ask five colleagues what the most limiting thing about your organisation is. You will get
five confident, different answers.

On what basis do you decide who is right? Seniority? Confidence? Volume? Most organisations
settle it with some blend of authority, anecdote and whoever tells the best story — which is
to say they don't settle it. They guess. Then they spend a year, a budget and a good deal of
everyone's goodwill finding out whether the guess was right.

That is not a management problem. It is a thinking problem, and almost nobody is taught the
technique.

So people invented one.
