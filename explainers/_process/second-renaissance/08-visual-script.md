# Somewhere to Put It — visual script

**Stage 2 of 3.** The approved script from `07-script-v2.md`, unchanged, with the
visuals annotated against it. Where a line here differs from the script, the script
wins and this file is wrong.

## The world

One continuous surface: **a working sheet on which a conversation is slowly becoming a
map.** Three objects recur and are never redrawn as something else.

- **Maren's card.** A small note, always the same size and shape, always `--mark`. It
  is the only thing in the piece that carries the accent, and it earns it: it *is* the
  thing at issue. It enters the thread, drains to `--ink-faint` when the group loses
  it, has nowhere to attach in the middle of the piece, and is re-lit only when it
  finally lands on a node.
- **The thread.** A vertical column of grey bars with dates down its left edge. Filed
  by time. Never by subject.
- **The map.** The series lattice — same node geometry everywhere it appears, so the
  reader reads it as one object seen repeatedly rather than as several diagrams.

Register is the series register: hand-drawn on paper, `#rough` displacement filters,
Organic tokens via `scroller.css`, serif running text, small sans caps for labels.

Nine pinned stages. Each needs its own portrait arrangement; the default move, as in
the trilogy, is to stack and carry sideways relationships on a vertical spine.

## What the drawings must carry that the prose does not

1. **The colour drains.** In the cold open the prose says a year passed and nothing
   changed. The drawing shows her card lose its accent and become one more grey bar in
   a column of grey bars. Nowhere does the prose say the idea was lost; the reader
   watches it go.
2. **The person who does not climb.** The prose lists four reasonable requirements.
   The drawing makes them a staircase between Maren and the map, restrikes them in the
   accent, and leaves her at the bottom of it with a crowd behind her.
3. **The condition nobody is standing under.** The closing stage puts a small figure
   beneath seven of the eight conditions. The eighth is empty. The script's last
   argument — that the map shows you the work nobody is doing — is delivered entirely
   as an absence, which only a picture can do.

## Sequence

### S1 · Cold open — pinned, 4 steps

The thread column is drawn from the first frame: eight grey bars, dates down the left.
Step 1 puts Maren at the left of it. Step 2 drops her card into the column in
`--mark`. Step 3 fans nine short reply bars beneath it. Step 4 **removes the accent**:
her card restrikes in `--ink-faint`, indistinguishable from the rest, and a faint
date label a year later appears at the foot of the column.

> Call her Maren. Eleven years on a smallholding in Devon, and she reads more theory
> than most of the people who write it.
>
> She joins the Second Renaissance forum and posts one paragraph: all of this floats
> free of any actual place, and none of it survives a bad harvest.
>
> Nine people reply. It is a genuinely good conversation.
>
> A year later, not one sentence of it has changed what the project believes.

### Title card — flow

Kicker: *The Second Renaissance · why any of this matters*. H1 **Somewhere to Put It**.
Dek: the script's italic line.

### Nut-graph island — flow, three paragraphs

The only region of ordinary prose density in the piece, per the form. Runs from *The
Second Renaissance is a project for civilizational renewal* to *…or of ideas.*

### Statement — full screen

> A shortage of somewhere to put them.

### S2 · Three times — pinned, 4 steps

A time line runs across the foot of the frame with three round-table marks on it (a
ring of six dots). Above each mark the same card rises in `--mark` and then falls back
to the line and greys — twice for the circle, the third being Maren's post. Steps 1–3
build the three risings; step 4 draws a dashed span beneath all three, labelled *the
same conversation, three times*.

> Maren's objection was not new. A research circle meets every week, and twice in three
> years it has arrived at her point on its own.
>
> Both times the room agreed. The work has been too abstract; it has to be rooted in
> actual places, with actual watersheds and neighbours.
>
> Both times, two weeks later, the agreement had nobody's name on it and nothing had
> changed.
>
> Anyone who has been in this project more than a year has had that conversation three
> times.

### S3 · What compounds and what doesn't — pinned, 3 steps

Same frame, same time line, teeth still present. Step 1 draws an ink staircase
climbing steadily across the whole span and never coming down, labelled *what a person
takes away*. Step 2 leaves it. Step 3 dims the staircase to `--ink-faint` and holds
the three fallen teeth at full strength — the group's version, back at the baseline
three times.

> It would be easy to call that a failure of follow-through, and it isn't.
>
> The people in the room learned something and kept it. They are better at this than
> they were three years ago. Individual understanding compounds — twenty years of
> anyone's students will show you that.
>
> What does not compound is the group's version. That has nowhere to live except in
> the people who were in the room, and people leave rooms.

### Statement — full screen

> Agreement that survives only in the people who were present is a rumour.

### S4 · No address — pinned, 4 steps

Two destinations, one on each side. Left: the thread column, dates emphasised in sans
caps. Right: a sheet of prose drawn as ruled lines, with one line struck in `--mark`
— the claim she is objecting to, present but unlabelled and unboxed. Her card sits
between them. Step 2 sends a dashed pointer to the thread; it stops short and ends in
nothing. Step 3 sends a dashed pointer to the marked line of prose; it slides past and
ends in nothing. Step 4 leaves the card stranded between two dead pointers.

> So where was Maren's paragraph supposed to go?
>
> The thread is filed by the day it was posted. Nothing in it is filed by what it is
> about.
>
> The papers are prose, and a claim in the middle of a paragraph has no address. You
> cannot point at it. If you cannot point at it, you cannot object to it precisely —
> and a vague objection to a long document is one nobody can act on, so in the end
> nobody makes it.
>
> Which means the most valuable thing a newcomer brings, a precise disagreement, is
> the thing the project is least able to receive.

### S5 · Five diagrams, and what a claim gets — pinned, 4 steps

Step 1: five sheets fan in, labelled GOAL · REALITY · TENSIONS · FUTURE · SEQUENCE.
Step 2: the fan recedes to `--ink-faint` and the lattice is drawn at full size — one
goal node, a row of condition boxes beneath, arrows between. Step 3: the arrows are
restruck heavier with a single small label, *is necessary for*, so the reader sees
that the lines are claims too. Step 4: two of the eight boxes fill with their words;
the other six keep their boxes and stay wordless, which is the shape of the rest
without the list.

> Earlier this year it tried something about that.
>
> It took its own written corpus and rewrote the strategy as five linked diagrams: the
> goal and what it depends on, what is blocking it now, the tensions underneath that,
> the future being aimed at, and the order things have to happen in.
>
> The point is not that they are diagrams. It is that every claim has an address, and
> the lines between claims are claims too — *this one is necessary for that one* — so
> the reasoning is exposed and not only the conclusions.
>
> Eight things have to be true at once for a Second Renaissance to be more than an
> idea. People have to understand the crises as one crisis. Small dense groups have to
> make the new way of living actually livable. Six more like that, up through
> institutions and the wise navigation of collapse.

### Companion pointer — flow, italic, one line

> *The method behind the five shapes is forty years old and was invented for factories.
> A companion piece covers it properly. Nothing here depends on it.*

Links to `../03-five-shapes/index.html`.

### S6 · A destination — pinned, 2 steps

The same lattice, same geometry. Step 1: her card travels to the *small dense groups*
condition and attaches to it, **re-lit in `--mark`** for the first time since the cold
open. Step 2: the single arrow feeding that condition is struck through in `--mark`,
and the two nodes downstream of it drop to `--ink-faint` — what else moves if the
arrow gives way.

> Maren's objection now has a destination. Rooted in an actual place is not a mood she
> shares with the project; it is a condition sitting under one of those eight, and if
> it is missing, it is missing at a spot you can put your finger on.
>
> And her disagreement gets smaller as it gets sharper. She does not have to reject the
> Second Renaissance in order to think it is wrong about how those small dense groups
> form. She can attack one arrow and leave the rest standing, and everyone can see what
> else moves if the arrow gives way.

### S7 · The stack — pinned, 5 steps

Maren at the foot of a four-riser staircase; the lattice at the top of it, out of
reach. Risers label in on steps 2–3: *read five diagrams · learn the grammar · find
your point · defend it*. Step 4 restrikes the whole staircase in `--mark` — reasonable
and fatal, and the accent moving onto the obstacle is the point. Step 5 fills the
ground behind her with three rows of faint figures, none of them climbing.

> Now ask what it actually takes, today, to move a single claim on those maps.
>
> Read five diagrams. Learn a grammar — what makes a cause sufficient, what makes a
> condition necessary, how to raise a reservation properly. Find your point among a
> hundred boxes. Then defend it to the people who drew them.
>
> Every one of those steps is reasonable. The stack is fatal.
>
> The maps fix the forum's problem by importing a worse one. They ask the person
> contributing to do the filing, and Maren is not going to do the filing. She has four
> acres.
>
> The project's own list of symptoms already has the line for it: *many people resonate
> with the ideas; few enter sustained practice.*

### S8 · The conflict — pinned, 6 steps

The conflict-resolution diagram as drawn in the working document: objective at the
left, two requirements, two opposed prerequisites at the right, built one side at a
time. Step 4 puts the lightning bolt between the two prerequisites. Step 5 draws the
assumption box across the middle in `--mark`. Step 6 cracks it.

> Underneath sits a conflict that every community which gives itself structure meets
> eventually.
>
> Some people want the focus — the clarity, the sense that effort is finally adding up.
>
> Others want the conversation exactly as it is, spontaneous and relational, and are
> rightly suspicious of anything that interrupts a good exchange in order to file it.
>
> Both wants serve real needs, and both needs serve the same goal. So the move is not
> to pick a side. It is to find the assumption that makes them look opposed, write it
> down, and check whether it is true.
>
> Here it is. *Structure has to be added by the contributor, at the moment of
> contributing.*
>
> That is the only thing holding the conflict together, and it is not true.

### S9 · Separate the acts — pinned, 4 steps

Three bands. Top: the conversation as a continuous wave that begins before the frame
and leaves it on the right, with Maren beneath it. Step 2: a band across the middle,
*reads afterwards, and proposes addresses*, with a dashed feed down from the wave.
Step 3: the lattice at the foot, and three ringed dots beside it, *who merges*. Step
4: her card lands on the node in `--mark`, and a dashed line runs all the way back to
Maren, labelled *and watches it land*.

> Let the conversation be a conversation. Nothing interrupts it. Maren posts her
> paragraph where she was going to post it anyway.
>
> Afterwards a machine reads the thread against the maps and proposes addresses: this
> sentence supports that condition, this one challenges that arrow, this one is a claim
> the maps have nowhere to put yet.
>
> A rotating handful of people decide what actually merges, because one person quietly
> deciding what the map says is how you end up with a map nobody trusts.
>
> The cost of contributing falls back to one sentence, said where you were already
> saying it. The filing is somebody's job, and it is not the contributor's.

### Flow — two paragraphs

> Then the thread is doing two things at once. It is still a conversation. It is also
> how the thinking changes.
>
> Maren's sentence ends up hanging off the claim about small dense groups as a standing
> objection with her name on it. She has not joined a committee or learned a notation.
> She has changed what the project believes, at an address anyone can find.

### S10 · Both directions — pinned, 2 steps

The eight conditions in a row, her objection attached beneath one of them. Step 2 puts
a small figure under seven of the eight. The eighth stays empty and takes `--mark`.
Nothing labels it. The caption is the script's line and the absence does the argument.

> And the map reads in both directions. The eight conditions that tell her where her
> objection belongs tell everybody else which of the eight nobody is currently holding.

### Close — flow, then two statements

> None of this is finished. The maps are provisional and wrong in several places. The
> machine that reads the threads is a prototype. The rules for merging are three
> sentences long and have never been tested by anyone annoyed.
>
> The maps are public anyway.
>
> Find the claim you disagree with. Or find the one nobody is standing under.

Then, one per full screen:

> The Second Renaissance has never been short of thinking.

> It has been short of somewhere to put it.
