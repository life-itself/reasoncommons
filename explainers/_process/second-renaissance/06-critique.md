# Critique — *Somewhere to Put It*, script v1

Adversarial read, in the voice of a non-fiction editor who does not care that the
brief was hard. The stage-1 gate: this has to survive as *writing* before any visual
work starts.

## What is working — protect these in revision

- **The spine is right and it is one thing.** A shortage of somewhere to put good
  thinking; the map gives claims an address; the address is unusable by hand; so
  don't make it by hand. Every section is on that line. The previous draft was an
  episode; this is an argument.
- **"Agreement that survives only in the people who were present is a rumour."** The
  best sentence in the piece. Everything before it exists to earn it.
- **The honest objection is in the piece rather than in a footnote.** The staircase
  section concedes that the maps import a worse problem than they solve. A piece
  advocating for a method that admits the method currently excludes people is far
  more persuasive than one that doesn't.
- **The close lands the title** without announcing that it is landing the title.
- **No jargon debt.** *Throughput*, *undesirable effect*, *evaporating cloud*,
  *prerequisite tree* — none of them appear. A cold reader is never asked to hold a
  term they were not given.

## Fixes, ranked

### 1. There is no person in this piece. — the one that matters

The checklist question is *is there a person: named, specific, present*, and the
answer is no. "Somebody reads the papers on a Tuesday" is not a person; it is an
indefinite pronoun with a weekday attached. We never learn what they said. They
return at the end as "the person who posted on Tuesday", still empty, so the payoff
lands on nobody.

The plan rules out the real June episode and the real quotes. It does not rule out a
person. Build one properly out of the composite the sources section already declares:
give them a name, a place, one specific sentence they actually wrote, and let that
sentence be the thing we follow.

**Everything else on this list is smaller than this.**

### 2. One idea should travel the whole piece, and currently three do

The research circle decides the work "needs to be rooted in actual places". Later,
"bioregional practice" turns up as an example of finding where you fit. At the end, an
unnamed sentence from the newcomer lands on a claim. These are the same idea arriving
three times without the piece ever admitting it.

Make them one. The newcomer's sentence *is* the rooted-in-places objection; the
research circle has had that conversation twice before them and lost it both times;
the thing that finally lands at the end is the same claim, in the same words, at last
in a place where it stays. That converts a set of illustrations into a plot, and it
gives the reader something to recognise on sight.

### 3. The stakes are abstract, and after fix 1 and 2 they don't have to be

Right now nobody loses anything. With one person and one idea running through, the
loss is concrete and free: a good, specific, correct objection has been raised three
times by three people and dissolved three times, and the project has spent a year not
knowing something it was told a year ago.

### 4. Four signposts, and the reader is being steered

*"Start with the conversation, because that is where most of the thinking happens."*
*"So ask the plainer question."* *"Two things change … and they are worth naming
separately."* *"Now the honest objection, which is not a small one."*

Cut the first two outright — the hard cut between sections does that work. Keep at
most one, and only if the turn is genuinely counterintuitive.

### 5. Told conclusions

*"That second one matters more than it sounds. A group that can only agree or defect
has one setting. A group that can revise a specific claim can be wrong in public and
keep going."* The reader was already there; being marched through it is worse than
being trusted with it. Cut to one line or drop.

Same for *"But the maps are public, and being wrong in public is the point."* — the
preceding admission already says it.

### 6. The eight conditions are a list, and lists kill momentum

Fifty-five words of semicolons in a single sentence. Two named, then the shape of the
rest. The drawing can carry the full set later; that is exactly the kind of step the
prose is supposed to leave to it.

### 7. "Something reads the thread" — three uses of *something*

Being coy about the AI reads as evasion, which is expensive in a section whose whole
job is to make an aspirational mechanism feel plausible. Name it plainly once — a
machine that reads afterwards — and the vagueness stops being a tic.

### 8. The reader should finish with a word, and *address* is right there

A claim in a paragraph has no address; a claim in the map has one. It is used twice
and never given weight. Promote it: it is the concept the whole piece turns on, and
it is short, concrete and un-jargony.

### 9. The plan asks for two landings and only one is delivered

Answer B — *my sense-making refines the thesis* — lands hard. Answer A — *I can see
how to get involved and align myself with this* — is one paragraph in the middle and
is gone by the end. The close should carry both: the map is where you find the claim
you disagree with **and** the piece of work nobody is doing.

### 10. Minor

- *thesis* appears five times and is the most abstract noun in the piece.
- "in the register you were going to use" — cut; the sentence is already made.
- Sources says "Nobody is quoted here", which will be false after fix 1 if the
  composite's sentence is written as a quote. Say what it is: a composite, written.
