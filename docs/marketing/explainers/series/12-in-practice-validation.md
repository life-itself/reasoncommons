## Newcomer review: prepared, not run

First show part two's investigation and saved result to a small number of newcomers. Use their explanations to find a major comprehension failure before recruiting a larger round. Then aim for approximately six to eight newcomers across desktop and phone; include direct entry to parts two/three and readers who stop after part one. These are formative checks, not a representative sample or conversion estimate.

Begin with open questions, without saying what answer is wanted:

1. What happened here?
2. What did the assistant contribute?
3. What changed someone's mind?
4. What would still be there if they returned next month?
5. Which parts were invented, and which showed software actually running?
6. What would you do next if you wanted to try this?

Observe whether the reader can find the execution record and installation/start instructions without assistance. Only after the open answers, ask whether any exchange got ahead of its evidence, whether the companion competed with the story, what felt slow, and which person, image or insight they remember. Keep uncertainty, misunderstandings and negative reactions; do not score an explanation as correct because it repeats product language.

Use separate records for page/version, entry route, device, pages actually read, unprompted answers, assistance needed, remembered positives and revisions suggested. Avoid retaining identifying information unnecessarily. Compare the main patterns with the supplied reviews, while acknowledging that different readers and versions prevent a controlled before/after claim.

Release judgment should consider both sides: readers should explain one consequential intervention, the saved result, uncertainty and the practical next action, while retaining the story's human stakes and core insight. If the product becomes clearer but the story loses its force, revise again.
