## The Wrong Queue

A clinic opens at eight. By noon, the queue is outside.

Everyone who works there can tell you exactly where the problem is.

They are all pointing at the same desk.

They are all wrong.

Sam manages; Maria runs reception and follow-up; Tomasz triages; Dr Adeyemi consults; Leila runs the pharmacy. This fictional team discusses operational flow.

Maria runs the front desk. She works through lunch most days. The phone starts before the lights are on and there is a queue at her window from opening until close. When patients write in to complain, and they do, they complain about her desk.

She is, visibly and unarguably, the hardest-working person in the building.

Her effort matters. But faster check-in alone will not let the doctor see another patient.

Sixty people try to visit on a typical working day. The clinic completes twenty consultations. That output has barely changed in four years.

In this simplified model, every visit passes five stations in order. Each has a daily limit.

Check-in can process sixty people a day.

Triage, forty-five.

The doctor, twenty.

Pharmacy, fifty. Follow-up booking, forty.

Every other station can handle at least twice what the doctor can.

So the clinic treats twenty people a day.

Not forty — which is what it would manage if the doctor's station matched the next tightest one. Twenty.

Forty visit attempts end without a consultation.

One of them is a man in his sixties who has now come twice about a cough he has had since March. He waited three hours on Tuesday and left at four to collect his grandson. He will try again next week.

10,000

unsuccessful visit attempts a year, if this day repeats across 250 working days. Some people try more than once.

The clinic has just been given enough money to hire one more person.

Use the hire only to speed check-in: still twenty.

Triage: still twenty. Pharmacy: twenty. Follow-up: twenty.

Add useful clinical capacity and the number can change. That is the capacity this model is short of.

If the hire only speeds check-in, patients reach the waiting room sooner. The budget has moved the wait indoors.

The station that sets the number — the one every other station is waiting on, whether it knows it or not — has a name. It is the constraint. In this serial model, it is the station with the smallest capacity. In messier work, finding the limiting condition takes investigation.

When everyone is working flat out, is the organisation working at its best?

But look at what full effort at the front desk actually produces. Sixty people checked in, against twenty the doctor can see. The other forty do not vanish. They sit in the waiting room — and a waiting room is not output. It is forty afternoons, spent.

A station that isn't the constraint, running at maximum, manufactures work the constraint cannot absorb. In a factory that work is inventory, stacked and going stale. Here it is people.

So a well-run system needs most of its parts to have slack — deliberately running below their maximum — so the one part that governs everything can run flat out.

Full stretch does not tell us whether the clinic is working well. We need to know what the effort produces, and where more effort would change that.

We know it is the doctor. So hire another doctor.

Not yet. Watch her day first.

Dr Adeyemi has been here nine years. She stays until seven most evenings writing up notes she had no time to write in the room.

Of her eight-hour day, she sees patients for about six.

The other two go on finding records, printing forms and preparing information. Some of this work could be done elsewhere.

Six clinical hours, twenty consultations: eighteen minutes each on average, including the history and assessment.

If all two hours became clinical time, the same average rate would allow twenty-six complete consultations. That is a projection.

Six more completed visits a day could mean fifteen hundred a year, across 250 working days. A patient like the man with the cough could be seen sooner. But the work must be eliminated or someone must do it.

Before choosing the hire, find out how much time can actually be recovered, what preparation would cost, and whose work it would displace. A small reassignment may help. Recovering all two hours is not yet a result.

Then coordinate the other stations around the doctor. Reception should keep consultations supplied without building an avoidable indoor queue. That can make check-in look slower even while the whole clinic works better. Maria will need a way to explain the wait.

Spend the hiring budget after that check: on whatever demonstrably increases useful clinical capacity, which may be preparation support rather than another doctor.

Notes on sources

The argument comes from H. William Dettmer, The Logical Thinking Process: A Systems Approach to Complex Problem Solving (ASQ Quality Press, 2007), chapter 1, and behind it Eliyahu Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, set out in The Goal (1984). The chain-and-weakest-link argument, the claim that a system performing at its best cannot have all its parts performing at theirs, the five focusing steps (identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, repeat), and the observation that most constraints are policies rather than physical things are all Dettmer's, from that chapter.

Ashfield is fictional. Its five serial capacities are 60, 45, 20, 50 and 40 visits/day, with illustrative demand of 60 attempts/day and 250 working days/year. Forty unsuccessful attempts × 250 gives 10,000 attempts, not unique people. Six clinical hours / twenty visits = eighteen minutes per visit; 480 / 18 = 26.67, or twenty-six complete visits if all two preparation hours can be recovered without another loss. Neither that recovery nor a fifty-visit doctor is an observed result. At hypothetical doctor capacity 50, follow-up 40 limits first, then triage 45; after both exceed 50, doctor and pharmacy jointly limit at 50. The simple model assumes the stated task capacities can be achieved together under the rota, and omits branching routes and variability.

Dettmer's own worked example of a non-profit system is, as it happens, a hospital: people in, healthier people out. The coral-reef image is adapted from John Gardner, quoted in the same chapter.

So here are the rules. They are less a technique than a set of questions you are allowed to ask about any claim anybody makes.

Do I understand you? Not do I agree — do I know what you mean. A great many arguments that look like disagreement are two people using one word differently and getting steadily angrier about it.

Is that a claim at all? A complete thought, one idea, that could be true or false. "Funding" is not a claim.

Is that cause enough on its own? If we have fuel and a heat source, then we have fire. True? No. You forgot oxygen — because it is always there.

Could something else have done it? The same effect can have several causes. Removing one does not necessarily remove the others.

Have you got it backwards? Anglers on the bank did not cause good fishing. They are how you know it. The cause is that the river was stocked on Tuesday.

If you’re right, what else would be true? If a later budget cut created this queue, comparable records from before the cut should not show the same longstanding queue.

And are you going in a circle?

Notes on sources

The five tools are H. William Dettmer's, in The Logical Thinking Process: A Systems Approach to Complex Problem Solving (ASQ Quality Press, 2007): the Intermediate Objectives Map (chapter 3), the Current Reality Tree (chapter 4), the Evaporating Cloud (chapter 5), the Future Reality Tree (chapter 6) and the Prerequisite Tree (chapter 7). Their names are omitted here in favour of the questions they answer; anyone going on to use the method will meet the terms soon enough.

The retired sixth tool is the Transition Tree. Dettmer writes in chapter 7 that over ten years of teaching he found it "the least valuable of the Thinking Process tools for nearly all practitioners," and replaces it with a more detailed Prerequisite Tree, critical-chain project management, and a chapter on the human element in change. The observation that an unstated goal reduces the question of what to change to "a matter of opinion and speculation" is from chapter 3; that conflict is "generally rooted in the hidden, underlying assumptions operating on each side" is from chapter 5; "ideas are not solutions" is from chapter 1.

The entire Ashfield case is fictional, including its observations, two-week trial, mixed follow-up and conversations. It illustrates how to record a prediction and review a limited comparison, not evidence of clinical improvement or a recommended appointment policy. Prepared existing information is distinct from new clinical documentation; necessary repeat visits remain part of care. No clinical outcome or permanent-hire decision is established. The separate execution record documents actual software/helper behavior during this revision.

The conversations are invented; no patient information is used. Desktop scenes illustrate the installed skill; phone scenes illustrate an explicitly invoked team-chat prototype. Proposed means not endorsed. Accepted for now names a person willing to work from the claim; it is neither proof nor group consensus.
