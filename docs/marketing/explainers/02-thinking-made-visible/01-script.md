# The Arrows Nobody Checks — script v1

**Stage 1 of 3.** Pure narrative. No visual direction. Judged as writing only.

Answers: why would you ever draw your reasoning out on a page?
Hands off with: one drawing isn't enough — different questions need different shapes.

Sources: Dettmer ch2 (Categories of Legitimate Reservation). Examples marked below are
his: the oxygen case, the fishing case, the appendicitis case.

~1,150 words.

---

At the clinic, five people are asked what is really holding the place back.

The practice manager says it's funding. A nurse says it's the ten-minute appointment rule.
Maria says it's the referral backlog. The doctor says it's the countersignature policy. A
receptionist who has been there eleven years says it's that nobody ever asks the people who
work here.

All five are intelligent. All five have been there for years. All five are certain.

Now: which one is right?

---

You cannot answer that by having them argue, because you have all been in that meeting. The
answer will be whoever is most senior, most confident, or least tired at four o'clock.

But notice something odd. Each of those five people is carrying, in their head, a chain of
reasoning. *The countersignature policy means every prescription waits for me, which means
I'm interrupted eleven times a morning, which means appointments overrun, which means we see
fewer people.*

That is an argument. It has parts. It could be wrong in specific places.

And nobody can see it. Not even the person holding it.

---

Try it yourself. Take something you believe about why something in your life isn't working.
Something specific — why the team misses deadlines, why the meetings are useless, why you
never get to the gym.

Now write it down as a sentence: *X causes Y.*

Two things tend to happen. The belief that felt crisp in your head goes vague the moment it
has to survive on paper. And once it's written, it starts sprouting doubts you had never
consciously entertained while it was living comfortably inside your skull.

Nothing has changed except that it is now outside you, where it can be looked at.

---

Most of our thinking about complicated situations happens invisibly, in intuition. Intuition
is fast, rich, and often right. It is also unexaminable.

When your instinct says *the real problem here is funding*, there is no chain of inference
to inspect, because there is no chain. There is a feeling with a conclusion attached. And so
disagreements between intuitions cannot be resolved by evidence — only by rank, charisma or
exhaustion.

Which is exactly what we saw at the clinic. Five feelings, no way to choose.

The fix sounds almost too simple. Write the reasoning down — not the conclusion, the
reasoning. Every step, every *this leads to that*, as statements with arrows between them.

---

Now, plenty of people draw diagrams. Flowcharts. Mind maps. Fishbone diagrams. Boxes on a
whiteboard with arrows between them.

Almost none of it helps, and it's worth being precise about why.

In an ordinary diagram, the arrows are unchallengeable. Nobody has ever, in the history of
mind maps, said *I don't accept that arrow*. They aren't claims. They're decoration — a
picture of what somebody already believed, drawn tidily.

An arrow is only worth drawing if it can be **wrong**. And it can only be wrong if there are
rules for what makes an arrow right.

That is the entire difference between a diagram and an argument.

---

So here are the rules. There are eight of them, and they are less a technique than a set of
questions you are allowed to ask about any claim anybody makes.

**Do I understand you?** Not do I agree — do I know what you mean. This one comes first, and
it comes first deliberately, because most arguments that look like disagreement are two
people using a word differently and getting steadily angrier about it.

**Does that thing exist?** Is the statement even a claim — a complete thought, one idea, that
could be true or false? "Funding" is not a claim. "Our budget fell 12% in real terms since
2019" is.

**Does that really cause that?** Say it aloud with the words in: *if funding falls, then we
see fewer patients.* Out loud, in front of someone, a surprising number of arrows collapse
on their own.

**Is it enough on its own?** This is the most common flaw in human reasoning, and Dettmer's
example for it is perfect. *If we have fuel and a heat source, then we have fire.* True? No.
You forgot oxygen. You forgot it because it is always there — so invisible that it never
occurred to you to say it. Every argument has an oxygen: the condition so ordinary that
nobody names it, right up until the day it isn't there.

**Could something else have done it?** A fever can come from an infection or from an
afternoon in the sun. If you'd removed your stated cause, would the effect have happened
anyway? If yes, you haven't explained anything yet.

**Have you got it backwards?** This one is worth slowing down for.

*Many fishermen are on the riverbank. Their nets are full. Therefore the fishing is good.*

That is precisely wrong, and almost everybody nods it through. Anglers on the bank did not
*cause* good fishing. They are how you know the fishing is good. The actual cause is that
the river was stocked on Tuesday.

Confusing the sign for the cause is how organisations end up managing their indicators
instead of their problems — hiring more receptionists because the queue is at the desk.

**If you're right, what else would be true?** Suppose the pain in your abdomen is
appendicitis. Then we should also expect a fever and a raised white cell count. If those
aren't there, it isn't appendicitis, however confident you were. This is the test that turns
an assertion into something that can be checked — because it makes a prediction that could
fail.

**And are you going in a circle?** *We're behind because we're slow. We're slow because
we're behind.*

---

Eight questions. None of them requires any expertise in the subject.

That last point is easy to skate past and it is the whole trick. You do not need to know
anything about running a clinic to ask *is that enough on its own?* You are not challenging
the person's knowledge, which you don't have. You are challenging the structure of the
argument, which is on the page in front of both of you.

---

And this is where something changes that has nothing to do with logic.

When reasoning is invisible, criticising someone's conclusion means criticising *them* —
their judgement, their competence, their eleven years of experience. So they defend, and you
push, and the meeting generates heat instead of light.

When the reasoning is out on the page, the page takes the criticism. Two people can stand
side by side looking at the same diagram, hunting for the flaw together. Dettmer's phrase for
it: not *you against me*, but **you and I against the problem**.

*I don't see how this cause is sufficient — what am I missing?* is not an insult. It is a
move in a game both players agreed to play.

And when a group has genuinely tried to break a chain of reasoning and failed, something
rare happens: they believe it. Not because they were told, but because they attacked it and
it held. People act on conclusions they have tested in a way they never act on conclusions
they have merely been handed.

---

Back to the clinic, and the five certain people.

You do not need to decide who is right. You need each of them to put their chain on the
wall, and then everybody gets the eight questions.

Some chains will not survive contact. One or two will turn out to be describing the same
cause from different angles. And what is left is not five opinions. It is one map that five
people built and nobody could break — which is a different kind of object entirely.

But a map of what?

Because *why are we stuck* is only one of the questions you'd want answered. What are we
even trying to achieve? What should we do instead? Will it work? What will it break? What do
we do on Monday?

Those are different questions, and it turns out they need differently shaped answers.
