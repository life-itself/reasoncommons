Guided reasoning, in plain words

Untangle the problem you keep coming back to.

Reason Commons is an AI facilitator for recurring problems, stuck decisions and plans you aren’t sure about. It asks one useful question at a time, maps your reasoning as you answer, and keeps a record of what you’ve agreed, what’s disputed and what still needs testing.

The pattern

You’ve been here before.

Retro notes · third time this quarter

The fix that didn’t stick

The same issue is back in the retro. Every fix made sense when you made it. None of them reached whatever keeps causing it.

Decision log · open for six weeks

The argument on repeat

Two people you trust keep making the same points. Each is right about something. Six weeks on, the decision still isn’t made.

Plan review · approved

The plan everyone nodded at

It sounded convincing in the meeting. Nobody traced how the first step leads to the result, or what it might break along the way.

Six months later

The reason nobody kept

Someone asks why you chose this. The people who knew have moved on, and the thread that explained it is buried somewhere.

These have the same root. The reasoning underneath stays in people’s heads.

What leads to what, what each side needs, what has to be true for the plan to work. Nobody lays it out, so nobody tests it. Nobody records it, so every discussion starts again from the beginning.

Why AI hasn’t fixed it

Answers are abundant. Reasoning isn’t.

Ask a general-purpose AI and you’ll get a confident, well-organised answer in seconds. It reads well. It’s also built from the model’s assumptions about situations like yours, its guesses sit next to your facts without a label, and nothing records which parts you actually accept. Next week you’re back with the same problem and a slightly different answer.

A typical generic answer · illustrative

Release delays often come from scope creep, testing bottlenecks or optimistic estimates. Common fixes:

  1. Freeze scope earlier in each cycle
  2. Automate regression testing
  3. Ship unfinished work behind feature flags
  4. Track cycle time to find the bottleneck

Want a rollout plan for any of these?

Sensible fixes, drawn from teams in general. Nothing here checks what is actually happening on yours, or keeps track of what you agree with.

Why this exists

Clear thinking shouldn’t be a privilege.

The methods behind Reason Commons come from Eli Goldratt’s Theory of Constraints and H. William Dettmer’s Logical Thinking Process. Since the 1990s they’ve mostly been taught in workshops and consultancies, to people with the time and budget to learn them.

Reason Commons puts their questions into an ordinary conversation, in plain words. It keeps a record of who said what, and of what each person has accepted for now. It doesn’t claim to have the answers. It helps you find your own, test them, and change your mind when the evidence says so.

Today it’s a skill you add to Claude or Codex.

Good reasoning belongs to everyone.

A different way to work

Make the reasoning itself the thing you work on.

Reason Commons turns a conversation into reasoning you can see, test and keep. You talk the way you normally would. The structure builds up underneath.

Lay it out

What you say becomes clear, single statements joined by the connections you describe. Words like “usually” and “might” stay in.

Test it

Connections get examined one question at a time. Why would this lead to that? What else has to be true? Is it necessary, or only helpful?

Agree on it out loud

Everything starts as proposed, your own claims included. Nothing becomes accepted until someone says so, and objections stay on the record with their author.

Keep it

The reasoning is saved to a plain file you own, with a numbered snapshot whenever accepted reasoning changes. Pick it up next week where you stopped.

How agreement works

Everything starts in pencil.

Each statement and connection carries a standing, shown by its border as well as its label, so you can always tell what’s settled for now and what isn’t.

PProposed · CauseJon
Scope often changes late in the sprint.

Proposed. Captured but not yet agreed. Your own statements start here too.

AAccepted · ProblemMaya
Releases usually slip by about two weeks, sometimes more.

Accepted for now by Maya and Jon

Accepted for now. Someone said they’re willing to build on it. It isn’t a claim of proof, and it can be reopened.

DDisputed · CauseMaya
Late QA is what makes releases slip.

Objection (Jon): March shipped on time even though QA started late.

Disputed. Someone objected. The objection and its author stay attached until it’s resolved.

How a session works

Four moves, at your pace.

  1. Bring what you already know

    A symptom, a stalemate, a plan or a goal. There’s no template to fill in. The structure is chosen from what you say.

  2. Answer one question at a time

    Each turn ends with one focused question and the reason for asking it. Take a different direction whenever you like.

  3. Watch it firm up

    Your answers become statements and connections, proposed until you accept them. Untested links and open objections go on a short list of work.

  4. Leave with something to act on

    What you agree on, what’s disputed and the next test worth running, saved so you can pick it up again.

Where it helps

Bring it the thing you’re stuck on.

Four common starting points. Copy a prompt to use with the skill, or adapt it to your situation.

A recurring problem

Why does this keep happening?

Start with

Our releases keep slipping, usually by about two weeks. Help me work out why before we change anything else.

It might open with
Think of the last time it happened. What was the first thing that didn’t go as expected?
You leave with
The causes you’ve tested, the ones you accept for now, and the check that would change your mind.

A stuck disagreement

Why do we keep having this argument?

Start with

My co-founder wants to hire now and I want to wait until revenue covers it. We’ve argued for a month. Help me see what each of us actually needs.

It might open with
What do you think your co-founder is trying to protect by hiring now?
You leave with
Both positions, the need behind each, the assumption that makes them clash, and an option worth testing.

A change you’re about to make

Will this plan actually work?

Start with

We’re moving customer support to tickets only. Help me check whether it will really cut response times, and what it could make worse.

It might open with
If it works exactly as you hope, what would a customer notice first?
You leave with
How the change should produce the result, what else has to hold, and the side effects to guard against.

A goal that’s still fuzzy

What does good look like here?

Start with

I want our onboarding to be better, but I can’t say what better means. Help me pin down the goal and what it depends on.

It might open with
Picture a new customer’s first week once onboarding works the way you want. What’s different for them?
You leave with
A clear goal, the few conditions it depends on, and a check on each one that asks whether you could reach the goal without it.

Opening questions are examples of how the skill tends to start. Your session follows what you actually say.

What’s different

What it does that a plain chat doesn’t.

The skill follows facilitation rules written into it. These are the ones you’ll notice first.

It asks before it assumes

If your reasoning depends on something that’s missing, it helps you establish it instead of filling the gap itself.

Your qualifiers survive

“Sometimes” stays “sometimes”. A hunch isn’t quietly rounded up into a fact when it’s written down.

Its own ideas are labelled

When the AI suggests a cause or a connection, it says so, and the file records it as the AI’s, separate from what people said.

Silence isn’t agreement

Moving on, viewing the map or saying nothing never counts as acceptance. Only an explicit yes does.

Objections stay attached

A challenged claim is marked disputed, with the objection and who raised it, until you resolve it.

It finishes before it widens

Once there’s enough material, it works through untested links and open questions before asking you for more.

Under the hood

A careful method you don’t have to learn first.

Reason Commons draws on the Logical Thinking Process, a set of reasoning structures from the Theory of Constraints for mapping causes, resolving conflicts, checking proposed changes, clearing obstacles and sequencing action. The structures are thorough, and they usually take training to use well. You don’t need to know them to start. The skill picks the structure from what you bring, and its diagrams name each structure when you want to look closer.

  • Plain-text memoryOne YAML file holds the participants, statements, connections, standing and open work.
  • History you can trustWhenever accepted reasoning changes, the helper saves a numbered snapshot that is never edited afterwards.
  • Views from the fileDiagrams are generated from the saved reasoning, one standing at a time, with joint causes shown as AND.
  • Works where you doDiagrams render in apps that support them and fall back to readable text where they don’t.
reason-commons.yaml · excerpt from the example sessionschema_version: 1
links:
  - id: l3
    from: [c1, c2]            # two causes acting together
    to: e1
    relation: contributes_to
    status: accepted
    accepted_by: [maya, jon]
    contributions:
      - {by: assistant, attribution: assistant, mode: text}
  - id: l1
    from: [c1]
    to: e1
    relation: contributes_to
    status: disputed
    contributions:
      - {by: maya, attribution: explicit, mode: text}
    challenges:
      - {text: Late QA on its own didn't cause a slip in March., by: jon}

For teams that build with AI agents

Every change traced to the reasoning it serves.

When an AI agent edits your code, the reason for each change is easy to lose. Inside a repository, the skill has the agent show a change proposal before it edits anything: the reasoning the change serves, the files it will touch and how the result will be checked. The agent waits for your typed acceptance, which is recorded with your exact words, the scope and the time. A pull-request check then confirms that every changed file and reasoning update is covered by an acceptance on record.

Example, abbreviated The real proposal also includes a diagram of the tree updates.

1The agent proposes

Type: Change proposal

Proposal: release-scope-freeze · revision 1

Change: Add a scope-freeze date to the release checklist.

Files: docs/release-checklist.md, reason-commons.yaml

Reasoning served: Future Reality · Injection · Proposed · f1: Freeze scope two weeks before QA on the next release. It addresses e1, the release slips.

Tree updates (proposed): Add Transition · Action · Proposed · t1: Add a scope-freeze date to the release checklist. Implements f1.

Validation: The next release records a freeze date, and its ship date is compared with the prediction in f2. One release can support the explanation but can’t prove it.

Status: Awaiting your typed acceptance before implementation, because changes to project files need a recorded approval. Reply to accept this version, or say what to change.

“Injection” is the method’s word for a change you propose. A transition action is one step that puts it in place.

2You accept, in your own words

Maya: Yes, accept release-scope-freeze
revision 1.

change_acceptances:
  - proposal_ref: release-scope-freeze
    proposal_revision: 1
    by: maya
    recorded_at: '2026-10-18T09:12:00Z'
    timezone: Europe/Berlin
    mode: text
    source: conversation
    source_text: Yes, accept
      release-scope-freeze revision 1.
    scope: Add a scope-freeze date to
      the release checklist, and the
      proposed action t1.
    node_ids: [t1]
    link_ids: []
    approved_paths:
      [docs/release-checklist.md,
       reason-commons.yaml]

The receipt keeps who accepted, what they typed, the scope, the files and when it was recorded. Approving the work doesn’t count as agreeing with the reasoning.

3The pull request is checked

$ check_pr_trees.py reason-commons.yaml --base origin/main --body-file pr.md

Tree check passed: serves 1 existing item(s); 1 tree update(s) match the YAML; recorded acceptances cover 2 changed file(s).

The check fails if a changed file or reasoning update isn’t covered by an acceptance on record, or if an earlier receipt was altered. It verifies coverage. It can’t prove who typed an acceptance or that the code does what was approved, so people still review the work.

Works in Claude Code and Codex. Comes with a pull-request template and a GitHub Actions workflow you can add to your repository once you point its paths at your copy of the skill.

Where this could go

A commons for reasoning.

Today Reason Commons is a skill you add to the AI assistant you already use. The idea underneath is bigger. Reasoning can become a shared resource that people build together, test in the open and keep, so decisions stay connected to the thinking behind them.

Available today In the skill

  • Guided sessions for one person or a group, in Claude and Codex
  • Proposed, accepted-for-now and disputed standing, with who said what
  • A saved reasoning file with numbered history and generated diagrams
  • Change proposals, recorded approvals and pull-request checks for code

Not built yet Possible next steps

  • Shared spaces where a team adds to the same reasoning at the same time
  • A searchable record of why past decisions were made
  • Automatic reminders to compare a plan’s predictions with what actually happened
  • Open commons where communities reason through contested questions in public

The “Not built yet” column describes directions the approach points toward. None of it is available today.

Questions

Before you try it.

Do I need to know the method?

No. Describe the situation in your own words and the skill chooses the structure. Its diagrams use the method’s names, so you’ll pick them up if you want to.

Isn’t this slower than just asking for an answer?

Each turn is a short exchange, and you can stop whenever you like. If you ask what to do, it recommends one next step with the reasoning behind it. The extra minutes go into reasoning you’ll understand, agree on and keep.

Can I use it on my own?

Yes. One participant is enough. In a group, say who’s speaking and each contribution is attributed to that person. Being listed as a participant never counts as agreeing.

Where does my reasoning go?

Into a plain-text file in your workspace, named reason-commons.yaml unless you choose another name, with snapshots and diagrams in a folder beside it. The skill has no server of its own. Your conversation runs in the AI assistant you already use, under that assistant’s terms.

What do I need to run it?

An assistant that supports skills: Claude with code execution turned on, Claude Code, or Codex. Python with PyYAML enables the diagrams and history snapshots.

What if it gets something wrong?

Correct it in plain words. Everything stays proposed until someone accepts it, and you can reword a statement, fix who said what or dispute a connection at any point.

Does it work for a whole team?

Yes, in one session with one person at the keyboard and the other participants named. Several people editing the same reasoning at once isn’t supported yet.

What does it cost?

The skill is a download with no separate account. Using it counts toward your plan with whichever assistant you run it in.
